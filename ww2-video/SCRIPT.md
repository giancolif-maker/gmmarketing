# SCRIPT v2 — World War II, three stand-alone episodes (~15 min each)

**What changed from v1:**
- **Each episode tells one complete story** with its own title, and ends on a cliffhanger into the next one. None of them is "part 1 of 3".
- **Hooks open on a question.**
- **Three running gags across the series:** America's shop, Mussolini's banners, and Klaus with the fuel gauge.
- **One gag that lives only in Episode 1:** the "Last One, Promise" form.
- **Cut from v1:** the Churchill tally and the Chamberlain mug. De Gaulle now gets one setup and one payoff.
- **Comedy comes from characters colliding,** not from the narrator commenting. No line explains a joke.
- **Every joke gets the "can I predict it?" test.** Predictable ones were rewritten as escalations.
- **Serious sections are told straight but stay visual:** maps, routes, documents and counters, never a static black screen.
- **Every number has been checked.** Simplifications are phrased so they stay true.
- **v2.2 surgical pass:** narration trimmed by about 15%, side trips compressed, Stalingrad made Episode 2's centerpiece, and most "that's real" certifications removed.
- **Production rule:** something visibly changes on screen every 5–10 seconds (a cut, a counter, a map move, a sticker or a character entrance).

**Hard rules**
- **No jokes about victims, ever.** The Holocaust, Nanjing, the Blitz, Leningrad, Warsaw, Hiroshima and Nagasaki are told straight. The next joke only comes after a scene change.
- **Satire aims at the people in charge.** Hitler is petty, vain and furious, never cool.
- **No swastikas or real Nazi insignia.** Germany's flag is a plain black-red bar.

## Running gags

1. **America's shop** (all 3 episodes). The sign changes over the series:
   - "WE'RE NEUTRAL! (Cash. You carry it.)"
   - "Borrowing available"
   - "OPEN FOR WAR"
   - The shop grows into a factory the size of the screen.
   - "CLOSED" … "OPEN (Cold War)".
2. **Mussolini's banners** (all 3 episodes). He pre-orders a VICTORY banner before every campaign, and Enzo the printer has to fix each one:
   - VICTORY IN ETHIOPIA
   - VICTORY IN FRANCE (France had already surrendered)
   - VICTORY IN GREECE, then "(PENDING)", then HELP IN GREECE
   - a blank banner
   - Enzo fired, then rehired
   - The last banner is "PEACE".
3. **Klaus and the fuel gauge** (all 3 episodes). Every big plan of Hitler's gets "Sir…" from Klaus and a look at the gauge, which drops every episode:
   - ½ before Barbarossa
   - ¼ in the desert
   - "no" at the end of Episode 2
   - Episode 3: the whole Battle of the Bulge depends on capturing fuel.
4. **The "Last One, Promise" form** (Episode 1 only). The same scene plays for every land grab, escalating each time, until the Poland form arrives.

# EPISODE 1 — "How Hitler Got Away With It" (1918–1941)

**Spine:** a nobody loses a war → Germany collapses → he rises → everyone underestimates him → he keeps getting away with more → Poland → Europe explodes → he makes the biggest mistake of his life.

## F01 — Hook
[0:00–0:10, no narration, one hard cut every ~2 seconds: a bandaged soldier in a hospital bed, 1918 → a giant rubber stamp slams down on a treaty → a map of Europe turning dark from the middle out → arrows crashing into Poland → a dark concrete bunker door closing → SNAP back to the hospital bed, silence.]

    NARRATOR: In 1918, this man was a nobody. A corporal. He'd just lost a war, he'd temporarily lost his eyesight to poison gas, and he had no job, no money and no plan.
    NARRATOR: Twenty-seven years later, he'd be dead in a bunker under Berlin, and tens of millions of people would be dead with him.
    NARRATOR: So how does a nobody do that?
    HITLER: (from the bed, to nobody) Germany didn't lose. Somebody… made us lose.
    NARRATOR: Short answer: everybody let him.
[TITLE: WORLD WAR II — "How Hitler Got Away With It"]

## F02 — The bill
[Versailles. A German diplomat at a table. A French diplomat slides over an envelope.]

    NARRATOR: The winners wrote the Treaty of Versailles. For Germany: less land, a tiny army, all the blame. And a bill.
    GERMAN DIPLOMAT: How much?
    FRENCH DIPLOMAT: We'll send the bill.
[Two years later. A postman. The bill unrolls down the stairs, out the door, and into the street.]
    GERMAN DIPLOMAT: (reading the last line) A hundred and thirty-two… billion?
    FRENCH DIPLOMAT: Gold marks.
    GERMAN DIPLOMAT: Is this… in installments?
    FRENCH DIPLOMAT: Oh, many installments.

## F03 — Money breaks
[A Berlin café, 1923. A man orders a coffee. A price board behind the counter keeps flipping.]

    NARRATOR: In 1923, Germany fell behind on the bill. France occupied the Ruhr, Germany's industrial heart. So the government paid the striking workers there… by printing money.
    CUSTOMER: One coffee, please.
    WAITER: Five thousand marks.
[He drinks it. The board flips.]
    WAITER: That'll be eight thousand.
    CUSTOMER: I was drinking it!
    WAITER: And while you were drinking it, it got more expensive.
[Kids fly kites made of banknotes. A man wallpapers his kitchen with money.]
    NARRATOR: By November 1923, one US dollar cost four point two trillion marks. Whole life savings vanished.

## F04 — The beer hall
[A crowded Munich beer hall. Hitler standing on a table. Most people eating sausages.]

    NARRATOR: The corporal had joined a tiny, angry party, and found he had exactly one talent: shouting at rooms until they agreed with him.
    HITLER: And who is to blame for all of this?!
    MAN WITH SAUSAGE: Inflation?
    HITLER: No!
    MAN WITH SAUSAGE: The… treaty?
    HITLER: Closer!
    MAN WITH SAUSAGE: (to his friend) I just came for the sausage.
    NARRATOR: He blamed Jews, communists and the treaty for everything. In 1923 he tried to seize power, failed, and went to prison. There, he wrote a book.
    PRISON GUARD: What's the book about?
    HITLER: Everything I'm going to do.
    PRISON GUARD: Should someone read it?
    HITLER: (smiling) Nobody will.

## F05 — The pitch
[A grey 1930s city, a long queue at a soup kitchen. Then a garish infomercial set: Hitler with a pointer and a phone number.]

    NARRATOR: Then, in 1929, Wall Street crashed and took the world economy down with it. By 1932, six million Germans were out of work.
    HITLER: (infomercial voice) Are you unemployed? Angry? Need someone to blame? Have I got a party for you!
    CROWD: How does it work?
    HITLER: Simple! Everything is somebody else's fault!
    CROWD: And then what?
    HITLER: (the phone number flashes) Vote now!
    NARRATOR: By July 1932, the Nazis were the biggest party in parliament.

## F06 — The box
[A government office. Three old conservative politicians in top hats stand around a cardboard box with Hitler inside it.]

    NARRATOR: In January 1933, a group of old conservative politicians had a plan: make Hitler chancellor, and keep him boxed in.
    CONSERVATIVE: Relax. We've boxed him in.
[Fire at the parliament building. Hitler's arm comes out of the box.]
    NARRATOR: A month later, the parliament building burned, and Hitler used it to suspend civil rights.
    CONSERVATIVE: We've… mostly boxed him in.
[Hitler's legs come out. A paper labeled "EVERY OTHER PARTY: BANNED".]
    NARRATOR: Then parliament voted him the power to make laws on his own, and every other party was banned.
    CONSERVATIVE: (holding a smaller box) This is a slightly smaller box.
[Hitler steps out entirely. He is holding the box. The conservatives are inside it.]
    NARRATOR: In 1934, he had his rivals inside his own party murdered, and when the old president died, he made himself Führer. Total power.
    CONSERVATIVE: (from inside the box) We would like to talk about the box.
[Cutaway: a family around a cheap little radio.]
    SON: What's on the other channel?
    FATHER: (turning the dial, pause) …Also Hitler.
    NARRATOR: And one by one, laws stripped Germany's Jews of their jobs, their citizenship and their rights.

## F07 — Mussolini's printer
[A Rome balcony. Mussolini posing. Below, ENZO at a printing press.]

    NARRATOR: Hitler had a role model: Benito Mussolini, Italy's dictator, who wanted a new Roman Empire. Mostly, he wanted it to look good.
    MUSSOLINI: Enzo! Print a banner! "VICTORY IN ETHIOPIA!"
    ENZO: We haven't invaded Ethiopia.
    MUSSOLINI: That's why I need it early.
    NARRATOR: Italy invaded Ethiopia in 1935, using poison gas on soldiers and civilians. The League of Nations, built to stop exactly this, barely did anything.
    ENZO: (hanging it) …Victory in Ethiopia.

## F08 — Tractors
[A German factory. Workers bolt a gun turret onto a vehicle labeled "AGRICULTURAL TRACTOR". KLAUS, a young quartermaster with a clipboard, is introduced.]

    NARRATOR: The treaty banned German tanks. So Germany's first tanks were officially called "agricultural tractors."
    NARRATOR: This is Klaus. Klaus handles supplies. Klaus reads the reports.
    KLAUS: Sir. This tractor has a cannon.
    OFFICER: It's for farming.
    KLAUS: Farming what?
    OFFICER: (pause) …France.
    NARRATOR: In 1935, Hitler stopped pretending. Britain and France complained, and did nothing.

## F09 — The form (#1: the Rhineland)
[A London desk. Two clerks, British and French. A crisp, neatly typed form slides in: "TERRITORIAL DEMAND. Is this your last one? ☑ Yes". A rubber stamp. After the stamp, a small sticker in the corner: "GOT AWAY WITH IT ✓".]

    NARRATOR: Then he started taking things, one piece at a time. 1936: troops into the Rhineland, which was supposed to stay free of soldiers.
    HITLER: Last one. Promise.
    BRITISH CLERK: Hm. Did he fill in the form?
    FRENCH CLERK: He filled in the form.
[STAMP: OK]

## F10 — The form (#2: Austria)
[Same desk. A new British prime minister, Chamberlain, umbrella. The form is now coffee-stained, the signature a scribble. Hitler slides it over without looking up. Sticker afterwards: "AGAIN ✓✓".]

    NARRATOR: In 1938, he annexed Austria.
    HITLER: (already walking away) Last one. Promise.
    CHAMBERLAIN: Splendid!
[STAMP: APPEASED]
    NARRATOR: Britain's new prime minister, Neville Chamberlain, believed in appeasement: give him a little, and he'll calm down.

## F11 — The form (#3: Munich)
[A big conference table in Munich. The form is crumpled, torn at one corner, "LAST ONE" scrawled across it in pen. Chamberlain carefully smooths it flat with his hand. A Czech official knocks on the window outside. Sticker afterwards: "AGAIN ✓✓✓".]

    NARRATOR: Then he wanted a large part of Czechoslovakia. Britain, France and Italy met him in Munich and handed it over. Czechoslovakia wasn't invited.
    CZECH OFFICIAL: (through the glass) That's my country!
    HITLER: Last one. Promise. Look, I've written "last one" in pen.
    CHAMBERLAIN: In pen! Splendid!
[STAMP: APPEASED]
    NARRATOR: Chamberlain flew home and promised "peace for our time." Six months later, Hitler took the rest of Czechoslovakia.
    CHAMBERLAIN: (searching the form) I don't see that on here.
    HITLER: That one didn't need a form.

## F12 — Kristallnacht (straight)
[A map of Germany and Austria. City after city lights up. On-screen counters climb: synagogues, shops, men arrested. No music.]

    NARRATOR: On the night of November 9th, 1938, Nazi mobs attacked Jewish communities across Germany and Austria. Synagogues were burned. Jewish homes and shops were smashed. Hundreds of people were killed or died afterward, and about thirty thousand Jewish men were sent to concentration camps.
    NARRATOR: It was called Kristallnacht, for the broken glass. Around the world, most governments condemned it, and still refused to take in more Jewish refugees.

## F13 — The worst friendship
[Stalin's office in Moscow. Hitler on a video-call-style screen. Both holding pens over the same document.]

    NARRATOR: Next: Poland. But next door was the Soviet Union, run by Joseph Stalin. Hitler hated communists. Stalin hated fascists. So they made a deal.
    STALIN: I trust nobody.
    HITLER: Same.
    STALIN: So we're agreed.
    HITLER: Completely.
[They sign. Under the table, each is already sketching an invasion map of the other's country.]
    NARRATOR: A promise not to attack each other, and a secret extra page: they'd split Poland down the middle.

## F14 — The form (#4: Poland)
[Chamberlain's desk. No form this time: Hitler drops a scrap of napkin. On it, scrawled: "LAST ONE? ☒ NO". Silence. Then the biggest stamp of the episode.]

    CHAMBERLAIN: (reading) "Not the last one."
    HITLER: I'm being honest now. Isn't that nice?
[Chamberlain slowly puts down APPEASED and picks up a different stamp: WAR.]
    NARRATOR: On September 1st, 1939, Germany invaded Poland. Two days later, Britain and France declared war. Two weeks after that, the Soviet Union invaded Poland from the east.
    STALIN: As agreed.
    NARRATOR: Poland fell in five weeks, and would suffer under both occupations for the entire war.
    NARRATOR: In 1940, Stalin's secret police murdered around twenty-two thousand Polish prisoners, many of them officers, in the Katyn forest and other sites, and blamed it on the Germans for fifty years.

## F15 — Waiting
[A trench in France. A British soldier and a French soldier playing cards. A tumbleweed. Then, inset: Finland in deep snow.]

    NARRATOR: And then Britain and France… waited. For seven months. Newspapers called it the Phoney War.
    BRITISH SOLDIER: Got any threes?
    FRENCH SOLDIER: Go fish.
[A calendar flips: October… December… March. The card game is now a tournament with a scoreboard.]
    BRITISH SOLDIER: (very tanned, very bored) Got any fours?
    NARRATOR: Meanwhile, Stalin invaded Finland, expecting a two-week war. The Finns, on skis, held out for more than three months, and more than a hundred thousand Soviet soldiers died.
    HITLER: (watching, taking notes) Their army is weak.
    KLAUS: Sir, are you sure that's the lesson?
    HITLER: Write it down.

## F16 — Around the wall
[The Maginot Line: a huge grey wall of forts on the French border. To its north, a thick forest labeled ARDENNES.]

    NARRATOR: France had the Maginot Line, a huge wall of forts along the German border. It stopped at the Ardennes forest, because everyone agreed the forest was too thick for tanks.
    FRENCH GENERAL: Tanks cannot get through the Ardennes.
[A German tank noses out of the trees. A cheerful TANK DRIVER pops out of the hatch.]
    TANK DRIVER: (waving) Sorry. Coming through.
[A second tank. A third. A line of them to the horizon.]
    FRENCH GENERAL: …Tanks cannot get through the Ardennes in these numbers.
    NARRATOR: May 1940.

## F17 — Dunkirk
[A beach. An enormous crowd of soldiers. Then the sea fills with boats: destroyers, ferries, fishing boats, Thames pleasure cruisers, lifeboats.]

    NARRATOR: The German tanks raced to the sea and trapped the British army, and many French soldiers, at Dunkirk. Then, for about three days, the German tanks stopped.
    KLAUS: Sir, they're escaping by sea.
    HITLER: Göring says the air force can finish them off.
    KLAUS: From the air, sir? An entire army?
    HITLER: He promised.
    NARRATOR: The air force could not. Remember that promise.
    NARRATOR: Britain sent everything that floated.
    DESTROYER CAPTAIN: Room for six hundred!
    FERRY CAPTAIN: Room for two hundred!
    FISHERMAN: Room for six! Mind the nets!
    PLEASURE BOAT OWNER: Room for four, and there are sandwiches!
    NARRATOR: In nine days, about three hundred and thirty-eight thousand soldiers were rescued. They left their equipment behind. They were alive.

## F18 — The railway carriage
[A railway carriage in a forest clearing. Hitler sits down, smug. A French tall general sneaks onto a boat to London.]

    NARRATOR: France surrendered in six weeks. Hitler made the French sign in the very same railway carriage where Germany had surrendered in 1918.
    HITLER: Same carriage. Same table. I've been waiting twenty-two years for this.
    AIDE: Sir, we have a war to run.
    HITLER: Same chair!
    NARRATOR: One French general escaped to London and announced on the radio that France was still fighting.
    DE GAULLE: I am in charge of France.
    BRITISH OFFICIAL: You're in London.
    DE GAULLE: I am in charge of France, from London.

## F19 — Late to the party
[Mussolini on his balcony, waving a sword. Enzo at the press.]

    NARRATOR: Mussolini saw France collapsing and jumped in, less than two weeks before the end.
    MUSSOLINI: Enzo! "VICTORY IN FRANCE!"
    ENZO: France already surrendered. To the Germans.
    MUSSOLINI: Then print it fast, before anybody notices.

## F20 — Churchill
[10 Downing Street. Chamberlain carrying a box out. Churchill coming in, carrying a much bigger cigar.]

    NARRATOR: In Britain, Chamberlain was out. On the day Germany attacked France, Winston Churchill became prime minister: the man who'd been warning about Hitler for years.
    CHAMBERLAIN: (passing in the doorway) You were right, Winston.
    CHURCHILL: Yes.
    CHAMBERLAIN: You could say "thank you."
    CHURCHILL: (sitting down) I could.
    NARRATOR: He offered Britain "nothing but blood, toil, tears and sweat."

## F21 — Carrots
[The sky over southern England, summer 1940. Spitfires. German bombers. A radar mast on the coast.]

    NARRATOR: Britain was alone. To invade it, Hitler needed the skies. But Britain had a secret: radar.
    GERMAN PILOT: How do the British always know where we are?
    GERMAN COMMANDER: Intelligence says… their pilots eat a lot of carrots.
    GERMAN PILOT: Carrots?
    GERMAN COMMANDER: For the night vision.
    NARRATOR: Britain spread the carrot story on purpose, to hide its radar. Germany lost the Battle of Britain, and the invasion was called off.

## F22 — The Blitz (straight)
[A map of Britain. Cities light up as they are bombed: London, Coventry, Liverpool, Glasgow, Belfast. A counter climbs. Families in an Underground station.]

    NARRATOR: Instead, Germany bombed Britain's cities, night after night, for eight months. More than forty thousand civilians were killed. Families slept in Underground stations. And Britain did not surrender.

## F23 — The shop
[A bright American storefront. Sign: "WE'RE NEUTRAL! (Cash. You carry it.)" Franklin Roosevelt behind the counter.]

    NARRATOR: The United States was neutral, and most Americans wanted to stay out of it. President Franklin Roosevelt wanted to help Britain anyway.
    FDR: Welcome! We're neutral. Cash only, and you carry it home yourself.
    CHURCHILL: We're out of cash.
    FDR: Oh. Then… borrow it?
    CHURCHILL: Borrow the tanks?
    FDR: Borrow them!
    CHURCHILL: And when do we give them back?
    FDR: Let's not think about that right now.
[The sign updates: "WE'RE NEUTRAL! (Borrowing available.)"]
    NARRATOR: It was called Lend-Lease: American weapons, food and fuel, now, with the bill later.

## F24 — Banner trouble
[A map: an Italian arrow into Greece bends backwards. Enzo at the press, three banners in progress.]

    MUSSOLINI: Enzo! "VICTORY IN GREECE!"
    NARRATOR: In 1940, Italy invaded Greece. Greece pushed the Italians back into Albania.
    ENZO: (adding a line) "Pending."
    NARRATOR: Italy also attacked the British in Egypt, who counterattacked and captured more than a hundred thousand Italian soldiers.
    ENZO: (crossing out a word) …"HELP IN GREECE."
    NARRATOR: Hitler had to send German troops to rescue him in the Balkans and in North Africa.
    HITLER: Again?
    MUSSOLINI: Thank you for your help in my victory.

## F25 — The plan
[Hitler at a giant map of the Soviet Union. KLAUS beside a fuel gauge, needle at ½.]

    NARRATOR: And now, the thing from the prison book. The thing he'd always wanted: conquer the Soviet Union.
    KLAUS: Sir. The Soviet Union is very big.
    HITLER: We'll be finished before winter.
    KLAUS: And we have half a tank of fuel.
    HITLER: We'll be finished before winter.
    KLAUS: And if we're not finished before winter?
    HITLER: (pointing at his notes) "Their army is weak."
    KLAUS: (quietly) That was Finland, sir.

## F26 — The warnings
[Stalin's office. A stack of letters piling up on his desk.]

    NARRATOR: Stalin received dozens of warnings that the attack was coming.
    AIDE: A warning from our spy in Tokyo.
    STALIN: Lies.
    AIDE: A warning from the British.
    STALIN: British lies.
    AIDE: And there's a German soldier here who crossed the border river tonight to tell us it starts at dawn.
    STALIN: (pause) …German lies.

## F27 — Dawn
[June 22nd, 1941, before dawn. A front line from the Baltic to the Black Sea lights up all at once.]

    NARRATOR: June 22nd, 1941. Nearly four million German and allied soldiers attacked along a front almost three thousand kilometers long. The largest invasion in human history.
    STALIN: (very quietly) I trust nobody.
    NARRATOR: Hitler had just attacked the largest country on Earth. Within six months, he would declare war on the richest one too. Over in America, someone was about to change the sign on the shop.
[END CARD: "NEXT: The Year Hitler Started Losing"]

# EPISODE 2 — "The Year Hitler Started Losing" (1941–1943)

**Spine:** in mid-1942 the Axis looks unstoppable, holding land from the Atlantic to the Pacific → three turning points in six months (Midway, El Alamein, Stalingrad) → by late 1943 it is losing on every front.

## F01 — Hook
[A world map, summer 1942. Axis territory spreads in a dark tide across Europe, North Africa, Asia and the Pacific.]

    NARRATOR: Summer, 1942. Germany rules Europe from France almost to Moscow. Japan has taken an empire across the Pacific in six months. The Axis is winning everywhere.
    NARRATOR: One year later, it's losing everywhere. And it all turns on three battles.
[Three empty boxes appear: TURNING POINT 1, 2, 3. Each one, when it fills later, gets a full-screen slam with a drum hit.]
    NARRATOR: But first: mud.
[TITLE: WORLD WAR II — "The Year Hitler Started Losing"]

## F02 — Mud, then ice
[The Eastern Front, autumn 1941. Tanks sinking in mud. KLAUS knee-deep, gauge in hand.]

    NARRATOR: Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union went terrifyingly well. Millions of Soviet soldiers killed or captured. By October, the Germans were nearing Moscow. Then the autumn rains came.
    KLAUS: Sir, the tanks are stuck.
    HITLER: (over the radio) Unstick them!
[Snow. The mud freezes solid.]
    KLAUS: Sir, good news. The mud is gone.
    HITLER: Excellent!
    KLAUS: It froze. The tanks are still in it.
    NARRATOR: The army had no winter uniforms. It had planned to be finished before winter. In December, fresh Soviet troops counterattacked outside Moscow and drove the Germans back.

## F03 — The parade
[Red Square, Moscow, November 7th, 1941. Snow. Soldiers march past Lenin's tomb, and keep marching, straight out of the city toward the front.]

    NARRATOR: Before that, with the Germans close to the city, Stalin stayed in Moscow, and held the annual military parade in Red Square anyway.
    SOVIET OFFICER: Comrade Stalin, after the parade, where do the troops go?
    STALIN: (pointing west) That way.
    SOVIET OFFICER: That's the front.
    STALIN: Yes. It's very close. Saves time.
    NARRATOR: Many of the soldiers marched from the parade straight into battle.

## F04 — The Holocaust (straight)
[A map of Europe. Deportation routes draw themselves as lines converging on Poland. Camp names appear: Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka, Sobibor, Belzec, Chelmno, Majdanek. A document: "Wannsee, 20 January 1942." A counter at the bottom of the screen. No music.]

    NARRATOR: Behind the front, Nazi Germany was carrying out the Holocaust.
    NARRATOR: In the occupied Soviet Union, killing squads shot Jewish men, women and children in mass executions, more than a million people by the end of 1942. In January 1942, Nazi officials met at a villa in Wannsee to coordinate what they called the "Final Solution": the murder of every Jew in Europe.
    NARRATOR: From across the continent, Jews were deported by train to death camps in occupied Poland. By 1945, about six million Jews had been murdered, along with millions of others the Nazis persecuted, among them Roma, disabled people, Soviet prisoners of war and Poles.

## F05 — Japan's whiteboard
[A Tokyo war room. TOJO at a whiteboard with a marker. YAMAMOTO, the only worried man in the room.]

    NARRATOR: On the other side of the world, Japan had been at war in China since 1937, a war that had already cost millions of Chinese lives, including the massacre at Nanjing. Then America cut off Japan's oil.
    TOJO: The plan. Step one: seize the oil fields of Southeast Asia.
    YAMAMOTO: Good.
    TOJO: Step two: destroy the American fleet so they can't interfere.
    YAMAMOTO: And step three?
    TOJO: (writing) Step three: America gets sad and goes home.
    YAMAMOTO: Have you been to America?
    TOJO: No.
    YAMAMOTO: I have.
    NARRATOR: Admiral Yamamoto predicted Japan could "run wild" for six months, maybe a year, and after that he had no confidence at all. Then he planned the attack.

## F06 — Pearl Harbor
[Sunday morning, Hawaii. A quiet harbor. Planes on the horizon. Then America's shop.]

    NARRATOR: On the morning of December 7th, 1941, Japanese planes attacked the US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor.
[Earlier that morning: two young radar operators in a hut on a hillside. A huge blip on the screen.]
    RADAR OPERATOR: Sir, there's a huge group of planes on the radar. Heading this way.
    DUTY OFFICER: (on the phone) Don't worry about it.
    NARRATOR: He assumed it was a flight of American bombers due in from California.
[The harbor. Explosions.]
    NARRATOR: Eight battleships were damaged or sunk. About two thousand four hundred Americans were killed.
    NARRATOR: But that morning, America's aircraft carriers were out at sea.
[FDR slowly turns the shop sign: "OPEN FOR WAR".]
    FDR: Yesterday, December 7th, 1941, a date which will live in infamy…
    NARRATOR: America declared war on Japan. And four days later, Hitler, who was not required to, declared war on America.
    KLAUS: Sir. Why?
    HITLER: Solidarity!
    KLAUS: With who?
    HITLER: (pause) …Japan?
    KLAUS: They didn't tell us they were attacking, sir.

## F07 — The factory
[The shop sign: "OPEN FOR WAR." The shop expands into a factory, then a factory the size of the screen. Workers, many of them women.]

    NARRATOR: America's secret weapon was boring: factories. Car plants built tanks. Millions of women went to work building planes.
    KLAUS: (reading) Sir, the Americans say they built ninety-six thousand aircraft this year.
    HITLER: Propaganda!
    KLAUS: There's a photograph, sir.
    HITLER: Fake!
    KLAUS: (turning it sideways) It's a very large parking lot, sir. Full of planes.
    NARRATOR: At home, about a hundred and twenty thousand Japanese Americans, most of them US citizens, were forced into internment camps. None was ever convicted of spying. The government apologized in 1988.

## F08 — Six months
[Map: Japanese red racing across Asia: Hong Kong, the Philippines, Malaya, Singapore, the Dutch East Indies, Burma.]

    NARRATOR: Exactly as Yamamoto predicted, Japan won almost everywhere for six months. Hong Kong. The Philippines. Singapore, where some eighty thousand British Empire troops surrendered, the largest surrender in British history.
    YAMAMOTO: (crossing off a calendar) Five months… six months.
    TOJO: We're winning!
    YAMAMOTO: (looking at the calendar) …Six months is up.

## F09 — The raid
[An aircraft carrier deck, much too short. A big twin-engine army bomber revving at one end.]

    NARRATOR: America needed good news. So in April 1942, sixteen army bombers, far too big to land on an aircraft carrier, took off from one, and bombed Tokyo.
    PILOT: Colonel, how do we land back on the carrier?
    DOOLITTLE: We don't.
    PILOT: Then where do we land?
    DOOLITTLE: China. Ish.
    NARRATOR: The damage was small. The humiliation was huge. It pushed Japan into its next gamble.

## F10 — The water trick
[A dark room full of codebreakers at Pearl Harbor. A blue ocean map with a tiny island: MIDWAY.]

    NARRATOR: June 1942. Japan planned to finish off the US Navy near a tiny island called Midway. American codebreakers knew an attack was coming on a target called "AF". But where was AF?
    CODEBREAKER: Tell Midway to send a fake message: "Our water machine is broken."
    NARRATOR: Japanese radio picked it up and reported: AF is short of water.
    CODEBREAKER: (calmly) Thank you.
    NARRATOR: American dive bombers caught the Japanese carriers with decks full of planes and fuel. In about five minutes, three carriers were burning. Japan lost four, and never took the initiative in the Pacific again. Two months later, the Marines landed on Guadalcanal, and started pushing back.
[Turning point counter: "1 of 3"]

## F11 — The other codebreakers
[An English country house. Huts on the lawn. Inside: people, machines, cups of tea. A U-boat captain at sea.]

    NARRATOR: In the Atlantic, German U-boats hunted the convoys Britain needed to survive. But at a country house called Bletchley Park, codebreakers, among them Alan Turing, were reading the German Enigma code.
    U-BOAT CAPTAIN: New orders. We will attack the convoy at—
    CODEBREAKER: (in England, reading) —grid square forty-two, at dawn.
    U-BOAT CAPTAIN: How did—
    CODEBREAKER: (sipping tea) —he know. Yes.
    NARRATOR: Thousands of Allied ships were sunk. But around three quarters of all U-boat crewmen never came home.

## F12 — The desert
[North Africa. Sand to the horizon. ROMMEL in goggles. KLAUS holding a jerry can. Gauge at ¼.]

    NARRATOR: In North Africa, Erwin Rommel, the "Desert Fox", chased the British deep into Egypt. The desert war was really about one thing.
    KLAUS: Sir. A quarter of a tank.
    ROMMEL: We'll capture British fuel.
    KLAUS: That's not a supply plan, sir.
    ROMMEL: It's worked before.
    KLAUS: Once, sir.
    NARRATOR: In late 1942, the British under Bernard Montgomery beat Rommel at El Alamein. Days later, Americans and British landed in Morocco and Algeria. By May 1943, the Axis was out of Africa.
[Turning point counter: "2 of 3"]
    MUSSOLINI: Enzo! "VICTORY IN AFRICA"!
    ENZO: (handing him a blank banner) Fill it in yourself.

## F13 — Spam
[An American cargo ship unloading in a Soviet port: trucks, boots, and crates and crates of tinned meat. Stalin inspecting a can.]

    NARRATOR: America's shop was supplying the Soviets too: hundreds of thousands of trucks, millions of boots, and a mountain of tinned meat.
    AIDE: Comrade Stalin, the Americans have sent… meat. In a tin.
    STALIN: I trust nobody.
    AIDE: It's called Spam.
    STALIN: (eating) …I trust Spam.
    NARRATOR: Years later, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev admitted that without Spam, they'd have struggled to feed their army.

## F14 — Leningrad (straight)
[A map: Leningrad cut off between German and Finnish lines. A thin line across a frozen lake. A bread ration card: 125 grams.]

    NARRATOR: In the north, Leningrad was besieged for almost nine hundred days. At the worst point, civilians got a hundred and twenty-five grams of bread a day. Around eight hundred thousand civilians died, most of them of starvation. Food came in over a road of ice across Lake Ladoga. The city never surrendered.

## F15 — The name
[A map of southern Russia. KLAUS points at oil fields in the Caucasus. HITLER points at a city on the Volga.]

    NARRATOR: In summer 1942, Hitler sent his armies south, for the oil fields of the Caucasus.
    KLAUS: Yes! Oil! Finally!
    HITLER: And that city.
    KLAUS: Why that city?
    HITLER: It has his name on it.
    NARRATOR: Stalingrad was a big industrial city on the Volga. It was also named after Stalin. Which meant neither dictator would ever let the other one have it.
    STALIN: Not one step back.
    HITLER: Not one step back!
    KLAUS: (looking between them) …This is going to be bad.

## F16 — Room by room
[A ruined city. A cross-section of an apartment building, German and Soviet soldiers on different floors.]

    NARRATOR: German bombers flattened the city. Then the army fought into it, street by street, house by house, floor by floor. The Soviets clung to a strip along the river, with fresh men ferried across under fire every night.
    GERMAN SOLDIER: We have the kitchen!
    SOVIET SOLDIER: We have the bathroom!
    GERMAN SOLDIER: Who has the stairs?
    BOTH: (looking at the stairs) …
    NARRATOR: The Soviet commander, Vasily Chuikov, had a rule for his men.
    CHUIKOV: Get closer to the Germans.
    SOVIET SOLDIER: Closer?
    CHUIKOV: Close enough to shake hands.
[A German dive bomber circles above two front lines a grenade-throw apart.]
    GERMAN PILOT: (on the radio) I can't bomb them. They're standing right next to our guys.
    NARRATOR: It went on like that for months.

## F17 — "Mostly"
[A Munich beer hall, November 1942. Hitler at a podium. Klaus in the wings, holding a field telephone.]

    NARRATOR: In November, Hitler told a cheering crowd in Munich that Stalingrad was as good as taken.
    HITLER: Only a few small pockets remain!
[Klaus's phone rings. He listens.]
    KLAUS: Sir. One of the pockets is the river.
    HITLER: Small pocket.
    KLAUS: And the factory district.
    HITLER: Small pocket!
    KLAUS: And the Soviet army, sir.

## F18 — The trap
[A map: two Soviet arrows punch through the Romanian armies on either side of the city and meet behind it. A ring closes.]

    NARRATOR: In November 1942, the Soviets struck the weaker Romanian armies guarding the flanks, broke through, and met behind the city. About three hundred thousand Axis soldiers were trapped.
    KLAUS: Sir, we're surrounded.
    HITLER: The air force will fly in supplies!
    KLAUS: Sir, that's the Dunkirk promise.
    HITLER: Göring promised again!
    KLAUS: The army needs about seven hundred and fifty tons a day, sir. The air force can deliver about a hundred.
    HITLER: So… nearly enough.
    KLAUS: (staring at him) It's one seventh, sir.
    NARRATOR: Hitler forbade a breakout. The trapped army starved through the winter.

## F19 — The promotion
[Hitler's headquarters, January 1943. A medal box. A radio. Then a cellar in Stalingrad: the German commander, Friedrich Paulus.]

    NARRATOR: By the end of January, it was hopeless. So Hitler had an idea.
    HITLER: Promote Paulus to field marshal. No German field marshal has ever surrendered.
    KLAUS: Sir, he's out of food. And ammunition. And army.
    HITLER: He'll understand the message.
[The next morning. Soviet soldiers at the cellar door. Paulus walks out.]
    KLAUS: Sir… he surrendered.
    HITLER: As a field marshal?!
    KLAUS: Brand new, sir.
    NARRATOR: The last Germans in the city surrendered on February 2nd, 1943. About ninety thousand German and allied soldiers went into Soviet captivity. Only around five thousand ever came home. The battle cost the two sides close to two million dead, wounded and captured. Germany's war in the East would never recover.
[Turning point counter: "3 of 3"]

## F20 — Kursk
[Open steppe. Rows of anti-tank ditches and minefields. German tanks, Soviet tanks.]

    NARRATOR: In July 1943, Germany tried one last big summer attack, at Kursk, with new heavy tanks. The Soviets knew exactly where, and were waiting behind belt after belt of minefields.
    KLAUS: Sir, they knew we were coming.
    HITLER: Our tanks are better!
    KLAUS: They have more tanks.
    HITLER: Ours are better!
    KLAUS: Ours are in the repair shop, sir.
    NARRATOR: Germany lost one of the biggest tank battles in history. From then on, in the East, it only went backwards.

## F21 — Enzo gets fired
[Sicily: Allied landing craft. Rome: Mussolini's balcony, empty. Enzo packing a box.]

    NARRATOR: That same month, the Allies landed in Sicily. Italy had had enough. Mussolini's own council turned on him, and the king had him arrested.
    MUSSOLINI: Enzo! Print "I'm still in charge"!
    ENZO: (holding his box) I've been fired, sir.
    NARRATOR: Italy surrendered in September. Germany occupied most of the country, broke Mussolini out of a mountain-top hotel with gliders, and set him up as a puppet in the north.
    MUSSOLINI: Enzo?
    ENZO: (sighing, unpacking the box) …I've been rehired.

## F22 — The firestorm (straight)
[A night sky over Germany. Bomber streams. A city map of Hamburg glowing red.]

    NARRATOR: Britain and America were bombing German cities, the British by night, the Americans by day. In July 1943, raids on Hamburg started a firestorm that killed around thirty-seven thousand people in a week. The bomber crews died in huge numbers too: more than fifty-five thousand from Britain's Bomber Command alone.

## F23 — Warsaw, 1943 (straight)
[A map of Warsaw. The walled ghetto drawn in. Dates. Then smoke.]

    NARRATOR: In April 1943, as the Nazis came to deport the last Jews of the Warsaw Ghetto to the death camps, a few hundred young fighters with pistols and homemade bombs rose up. They held out for almost a month. The Germans burned the ghetto to the ground. The uprising became a lasting symbol of resistance.

## F24 — The date
[Tehran, November 1943. Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin at a round table. A calendar. KLAUS in Berlin holding the gauge.]

    NARRATOR: In November 1943, Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin finally met, in Tehran. The Soviets were doing most of the dying: in the end, around twenty-seven million Soviet citizens died in the war. Stalin had one question.
    STALIN: When do you invade France?
    CHURCHILL: Soon.
    STALIN: You said soon last year.
    FDR: May 1944.
    STALIN: (writing it down) I trust nobody. But I'm writing this down.
[Berlin. Klaus looks at the gauge.]
    KLAUS: Sir. The gauge.
    HITLER: What does it say?
    KLAUS: (turning it around) It says "no."
    NARRATOR: The Allies were going to invade France. Hitler's generals thought they knew exactly where. They were wrong.
[END CARD: "NEXT: The Year Hitler Lost Everything"]

# EPISODE 3 — "The Year Hitler Lost Everything" (1944–1945)

**Spine (v2.4, bunker-framed):** Berlin, April 28th, 1945: Hitler has 48 hours left → ten months earlier: how do you break into a fortress continent? → D-Day → the race to Germany → Hitler's last gambles fail → the horror is uncovered → back in the bunker for the last 48 hours → Japan won't surrender → the bombs → a piece of paper.

**The frame:** the episode opens in the bunker, cuts back to June 1944, and returns to the bunker twice (a one-shot check-in after the Bulge, then the full finale). A clock card, "HOURS LEFT", is used only at these bunker moments. Everything in the last 48 hours is told straight, with no jokes from the moment the bunker finale begins until the scene changes to London.

## F01 — Hook: 48 hours
[Berlin, the night of April 28th, 1945. Shelling shakes dust from a concrete ceiling. A map table lit by one bulb, covered in pins for armies that no longer exist. HITLER, stooped, one hand shaking. KLAUS in the doorway, holding a radio message. A card: 48 HOURS LEFT. No music.]

    NARRATOR: Berlin, April 28th, 1945. Fifty feet under the Chancellery garden, Adolf Hitler is moving armies around a map. Most of them no longer exist.
    NARRATOR: Above him, the Soviet army is fighting street by street toward the bunker. He has about forty-eight hours left.
    NARRATOR: Ten months earlier, he still controlled almost all of Europe. So how did it fall apart this fast?
[Hard cut: rain on a window in England, June 1944.]
    NARRATOR: It started with the weather.
[TITLE: WORLD WAR II — "The Year Hitler Lost Everything"]

## F01b — The note
[June 4th, 1944. Rain on a window. A weather chart. A pencil. A folded piece of paper. Then hard cuts to the scale: soldiers, ships, planes, filling the screen.]

    NARRATOR: More than a hundred and fifty thousand soldiers, seven thousand ships and boats, and eleven thousand aircraft are ready to attack the most heavily defended coastline in the world. The plan depends on a fake army, and the weather. And the weather is terrible.
    NARRATOR: The man in charge, Dwight Eisenhower, has already written the statement he'll read if it all goes wrong.
[Close on the note: "Our landings… have failed… If any blame or fault attaches to the attempt it is mine alone."]
    NARRATOR: Here's why he never had to read it.

## F02 — The underbelly
[Italy: mountains, rain, mud. Allied soldiers struggling up a rocky slope. CHURCHILL on a phone.]

    NARRATOR: The Allies had already invaded Italy, which Churchill had called the "soft underbelly" of Europe. Italy is mostly mountains. Every ridge was a German defensive line.
    AMERICAN SOLDIER: (climbing) Soft?
    BRITISH SOLDIER: (climbing) Underbelly?
    CHURCHILL: (on the phone) …A mountainous underbelly.
    NARRATOR: Rome finally fell on June 4th, 1944. It was front-page news for exactly two days.

## F03 — The fake army
[A field in southeast England. Soldiers inflating rubber tanks with foot pumps. GEORGE PATTON in front of them, chin out.]

    NARRATOR: Hitler's generals expected the invasion at Calais, where the Channel is narrowest. So the Allies gave them an army at Calais: a completely fake one. Inflatable tanks, plywood landing craft, fake radio chatter, and a real, famous general in command.
    SOLDIER: (pumping) General, your tank is ready.
    PATTON: That's a balloon.
    SOLDIER: Yes, sir.
    PATTON: I am a fighting general and I command an army of balloons.
    EISENHOWER: The most important balloons in history, George.
    NARRATOR: Patton was free because he'd slapped two shell-shocked soldiers in Sicily and been benched. But the Germans couldn't believe the Allies would invade without their most famous general. So he got the balloons.
    NARRATOR: The real landing would be in Normandy, on open beaches. The Allies even brought their own harbors, built in pieces and towed across the sea.

## F04 — D-Day
[June 6th, 1944. Five beach names appear one by one: UTAH, OMAHA, GOLD, JUNO, SWORD. Paratroopers in the dark. Landing craft.]

    NARRATOR: The storm eased just enough. Eisenhower delayed one day, then said go. Paratroopers dropped in the night. At dawn on June 6th, American, British and Canadian troops hit five beaches.
    NARRATOR: At Omaha Beach, the defenses were still intact, and the Americans took terrible losses getting off the sand. By nightfall, all five beaches were held, at a cost of around ten thousand Allied casualties.
[Hitler's mountain house in Bavaria. A dark bedroom. KLAUS in the doorway.]
    KLAUS: (whispering) Sir. The invasion.
    HITLER: (under a blanket) Is it at Calais?
    KLAUS: Normandy, sir.
    HITLER: Then it's a trick. The real one is at Calais. Go away.
    KLAUS: (looking at the balloons on a map) …Yes, sir.

## F05 — Rockets
[London, summer 1944. A small pilotless plane with a buzzing engine. Two Londoners looking up.]

    NARRATOR: A week later, Hitler fired his "vengeance weapons" at London: the V-1 flying bomb, and later the V-2, the world's first long-range ballistic missile.
    LONDONER: As long as you can hear it buzzing, you're fine.
    OTHER LONDONER: And when it stops?
    LONDONER: (the buzzing stops) …
    NARRATOR: They killed thousands of civilians in Britain and Belgium. Thousands more prisoners and forced laborers died building them.

## F06 — Paris
[Normandy's high hedgerows. Then Paris: crowds, flags, flowers. DE GAULLE walks down the Champs-Élysées.]

    NARRATOR: Normandy's hedgerows made every field a fortress. It took almost two months to break out. Then the German army in France collapsed, and on August 25th, 1944, Paris was liberated.
    DE GAULLE: I am in charge of France.
    PARISIANS: (cheering, throwing flowers) Yes! You are!
    DE GAULLE: (stopping, stunned) …Finally.

## F07 — Bagration
[The Eastern Front map. A huge red wave rolls west through Belarus. A blue block on the map labeled ARMY GROUP CENTER.]

    NARRATOR: In the East, three years to the day after Hitler invaded, the Soviets launched Operation Bagration, and drove hundreds of kilometers west in weeks. It was a bigger German defeat than Stalingrad.
    HITLER: Where is Army Group Center?
    KLAUS: (pointing at an empty map) It was here, sir.
    HITLER: And now?
    KLAUS: Now it's a "was."

## F08 — Warsaw, 1944 (straight)
[A map of Warsaw and the Vistula river. The Red Army arrives on the far bank and stops. A day counter: 1… 63.]

    NARRATOR: In August 1944, with the Red Army approaching, the Polish Home Army rose up against the Germans in Warsaw. The Soviet army halted on the other side of the river, and for weeks Stalin refused to let Allied planes use Soviet airfields to help. The uprising lasted sixty-three days. Around two hundred thousand Poles were killed, most of them civilians, and the Germans then destroyed most of what was left of the city.

## F09 — The briefcase
[A wooden map hut in East Prussia. A briefcase under a heavy table. Someone nudges it with a foot behind a thick table leg.]

    NARRATOR: By July 1944, some German officers had seen enough. Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg carried a bomb in a briefcase into Hitler's meeting, set it under the table, and walked out.
    OFFICER: (nudging the briefcase behind a thick table leg) Sorry, is this yours?
[BOOM. Smoke. Hitler emerges: hair on end, trousers in shreds.]
    HITLER: I am… fine.
    NARRATOR: The thick table leg shielded him. The plotters, and thousands of others, were executed.

## F10 — A bridge too far
[The Netherlands. A single road running north across three rivers, three bridges. Paratroopers floating down. MONTGOMERY with a pointer.]

    NARRATOR: In September 1944, Montgomery had a plan to end the war by Christmas: drop paratroopers on a chain of bridges across the Netherlands, and race tanks up a single road to reach them.
    MONTGOMERY: One road. Three rivers. Sixty-four miles. Splendid.
    AMERICAN GENERAL: And if one bridge goes wrong?
    MONTGOMERY: Then it's… less splendid.
    NARRATOR: The tanks got stuck on the narrow road. At the last bridge, at Arnhem, British paratroopers held out for days, waiting for help that never came. A bridge too far.

## F11 — The fuel plan
[The Ardennes forest in snow, December 1944. KLAUS holding an empty jerry can. On the map, a big American fuel depot.]

    NARRATOR: December 1944. Hitler's last big gamble in the West: a surprise attack through the Ardennes forest. Again.
    KLAUS: Sir. The tanks have enough fuel to get about halfway.
    HITLER: They'll capture American fuel on the way!
    KLAUS: Sir, this is the desert plan.
    HITLER: It was a good plan!
    KLAUS: It didn't work in the desert, sir. And this is a forest.
    NARRATOR: It punched a "bulge" into the American line. At Bastogne, surrounded American paratroopers got a German demand to surrender. Their commander replied with one word: "Nuts!"
    GERMAN OFFICER: What does "nuts" mean?
    AMERICAN OFFICER: It means "go to hell."
    NARRATOR: Then the skies cleared, Allied planes returned, and the German tanks ran dry, some of them just short of the fuel depots they'd been aiming for.
    KLAUS: (looking at the gauge, then at the camera) …
[Hard cut: the bunker map table, the single bulb, the same pins. A card: 48 HOURS LEFT. A beat of silence. Cut back.]

## F12 — One bridge
[The Rhine river, March 1945. A railway bridge at Remagen, still standing. American soldiers staring at it.]

    NARRATOR: To get into Germany, the Allies had to cross the Rhine. The Germans blew up every bridge. Every bridge but one.
    AMERICAN SOLDIER: Sir. There's a bridge.
    OFFICER: There are no bridges.
    AMERICAN SOLDIER: It's right there, sir.
    OFFICER: (looking) …Run.
    NARRATOR: At Remagen, the demolition charges failed. Hitler had several of his own officers executed for it.

## F13 — The camps (straight)
[A map of Europe with camp names. Dates appear: 27 January 1945 (Auschwitz), 11 April (Buchenwald), 15 April (Bergen-Belsen), 29 April (Dachau). Film reels and notebooks on a table: the evidence being recorded.]

    NARRATOR: As the Allied armies advanced, they reached the camps. Soviet soldiers entered Auschwitz on January 27th, 1945. American and British troops reached Buchenwald, Bergen-Belsen and Dachau in April.
    NARRATOR: They found starving survivors, mass graves, and the machinery of murder. General Eisenhower ordered it all to be filmed and photographed, and brought in reporters and politicians to see it, so that no one could ever claim it hadn't happened.
    NARRATOR: Six million Jews were murdered in the Holocaust, about two out of every three Jews in Europe. Millions of others were murdered too.

## F14 — The secret
[A palace at Yalta. Roosevelt, frail. Then the White House: Harry Truman behind a desk, an aide with a folder marked TOP SECRET.]

    NARRATOR: In April 1945, Roosevelt died. His vice president, Harry Truman, took over, and learned the biggest secret of the war.
    AIDE: Mr. President, we're building a bomb.
    TRUMAN: What kind of bomb?
    AIDE: One bomb that can destroy a city.
    TRUMAN: And nobody told me?
    AIDE: It was a secret.
    TRUMAN: I was the vice president!
    AIDE: It was a very big secret.

## F15 — The last banner
[A lakeside road in northern Italy, April 1945. Then Enzo, alone with his press.]

    NARRATOR: On April 28th, 1945, the day this episode began, Mussolini was caught trying to flee to Switzerland in a German army coat. Italian partisans recognized him, and shot him.
[Enzo, alone, prints one last banner and holds it up: "PEACE".]
    ENZO: (looking at it) Huh. A true one.

## F16 — Berlin
[Berlin in ruins. Soviet tanks. Then the bunker: the map table, the bulb.]

    NARRATOR: Back to the bunker. By late April, the Soviet army had encircled Berlin, and Hitler was still sending orders to armies that no longer existed.
    HITLER: Send the Ninth Army!
    KLAUS: Sir, it's surrounded.
    HITLER: The Twelfth Army, then!
    KLAUS: It's mostly gone, sir.
    HITLER: Then who is defending Berlin?
    KLAUS: Old men and boys, sir. And me.

## F16b — The last 48 hours (straight)
[From here to the end of the scene: no music, no jokes, no character dialogue. The HOURS LEFT card counts down between beats: 48, 40, 24, 0. Slow, static shots: the radio message, a pen, a document, a corridor, a door, the garden exit, a map of Berlin with the Soviet line closing in.]

    NARRATOR: On the night of April 28th, a radio report arrived: Heinrich Himmler, one of his most loyal men, had secretly offered to surrender to the Western Allies. Hitler raged at the betrayal, and had Himmler's liaison officer in the bunker shot.
    NARRATOR: Just after midnight, he married Eva Braun, his companion of many years, in a small ceremony in the bunker. Then he dictated his last testament. To the very end, he blamed the Jews for the war he had started.
    NARRATOR: On April 29th, news reached the bunker that Mussolini was dead, and his body had been hung up in a square in Milan. Hitler ordered that his own body be burned.
    NARRATOR: On the afternoon of April 30th, with Soviet soldiers a few streets away, Hitler and Eva Braun killed themselves. Their bodies were carried up to the garden and burned. He never faced a trial.
    NARRATOR: The next day, Joseph Goebbels and his wife Magda had their six children poisoned in the bunker, then killed themselves.
    NARRATOR: On May 2nd, Berlin surrendered. Germany surrendered unconditionally on May 8th, 1945: Victory in Europe Day.
[The HOURS LEFT card reads 0, then fades. Hard cut to London crowds: the next scene.]

## F17 — The election
[London, May 1945. Huge crowds. Then July: a ballot box. Churchill packing a box. A polite, quiet man at the door.]

    NARRATOR: Victory in Europe brought millions into the streets. Two months later, British voters, who wanted homes, jobs and a health service, voted Churchill out.
    ATTLEE: (politely) Hello. I'm the new prime minister.
    CHURCHILL: You?
    ATTLEE: Me.
    CHURCHILL: We won the war.
    ATTLEE: And thank you very much for that.
    NARRATOR: He was back as prime minister six years later. He was very stubborn.

## F18 — I have returned
[A beach in the Philippines, October 1944. General Douglas MacArthur wading ashore in sunglasses. A photographer on the sand.]

    NARRATOR: Meanwhile, in the Pacific. General Douglas MacArthur had been forced out of the Philippines in 1942, and promised, "I shall return." In October 1944, he waded ashore.
    MACARTHUR: People of the Philippines, I have returned!
    PHOTOGRAPHER: Chin up, General.
    MACARTHUR: (lifting chin, to the camera) …I have returned.

## F19 — The Pacific
[The Pacific map. Island names appear: Saipan, Leyte, Iwo Jima, Okinawa. Then cities in Japan under bombers.]

    NARRATOR: Off the Philippines, the battle of Leyte Gulf became the largest naval battle in history. Japan began sending kamikaze pilots to crash their planes into American ships.
    NARRATOR: In 1945 came Iwo Jima and Okinawa. Tens of thousands of Americans, and far more Japanese soldiers and Okinawan civilians, died in those last island battles. American bombers burned Japan's cities. One raid on Tokyo in March killed around a hundred thousand people.
    NARRATOR: And Japan's leaders still would not surrender.

## F20 — The second flag
[Iwo Jima, February 1945. A small group of Marines raising a small flag on a mountaintop. Then another group raising a bigger one. A photographer.]

    NARRATOR: On the island of Iwo Jima, in February 1945, Marines raised an American flag on top of Mount Suribachi. Then they decided it was too small, and sent up a bigger one.
    MARINE: That flag's too small.
    OTHER MARINE: It's a flag.
    MARINE: People should be able to see it from the beach.
    NARRATOR: A photographer caught the second flag going up. It became one of the most famous photographs ever taken. The battle for the island went on for another month, and three of the six men in that photo were killed there.

## F21 — Potsdam
[Potsdam, July 1945. Truman, Attlee, Stalin at a round table. Truman leans over to whisper.]

    NARRATOR: In July 1945, the Allied leaders met at Potsdam. Truman had just learned that the atomic bomb worked.
    TRUMAN: (casually) We have a new weapon. Very powerful.
    STALIN: (casually) Good. Use it well.
    TRUMAN: (to an aide) He didn't even blink.
    NARRATOR: Stalin didn't blink because he already knew. Soviet spies had been inside the bomb project for years.
    STALIN: I trust nobody.

## F22 — The bombs (straight)
[A plain sky. Two dates. Two city names. No sound effects.]

    NARRATOR: On August 6th, 1945, the United States dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima. Three days later, a second bomb destroyed Nagasaki. By the end of that year, between a hundred and ten thousand and two hundred and ten thousand people had died, most of them civilians.
    NARRATOR: The same week, the Soviet Union declared war on Japan and invaded Manchuria. Emperor Hirohito decided to surrender.

## F23 — The record
[The Imperial Palace in Tokyo, the night of August 14th, 1945. Rebel officers running through dark corridors. A small phonograph record hidden in a cupboard.]

    NARRATOR: Even then, it almost didn't happen. The emperor recorded his surrender speech, to be broadcast the next day. That night, a group of army officers stormed the palace to find the recording and stop it.
    REBEL OFFICER: Find the record!
    OTHER OFFICER: Which room?
    REBEL OFFICER: All the rooms!
    NARRATOR: The palace staff had hidden it. The rebels never found it. On August 15th, Japan heard its emperor's voice on the radio for the first time, announcing the surrender.

## F24 — A piece of paper
[The deck of the USS Missouri, September 2nd, 1945. A table. A document. Then a quick flash: the hospital bed from 1918.]

    NARRATOR: On September 2nd, 1945, on the deck of the battleship Missouri in Tokyo Bay, Japan signed the surrender. The Second World War was over.
    NARRATOR: It began with a man furious about a piece of paper. It ended with a piece of paper.
    NARRATOR: Somewhere between seventy and eighty-five million people were dead, around three percent of everyone alive in the world. Most of them were civilians.

## F25 — The sign
[America's shop. Truman flips the sign to "CLOSED". A pause. Then a phone rings.]

    NARRATOR: Nazi leaders were put on trial at Nuremberg. The United Nations was founded.
    TRUMAN: (flipping the sign) Closed.
[The phone rings. Truman answers.]
    TRUMAN: …It's Stalin.
[He flips the sign back: "OPEN (Cold War)".]

## Production notes (animation)
- **Vary the composition constantly:** close-ups, wide maps, split screens, inserts. Never the same framing twice in a row.
- **Rhythm is story → tension → joke → escalation → visual gag → serious beat → payoff.** It should never become a fact → joke → fact → joke metronome.
- **Klaus is rationed.** Leave 30–90 seconds between his appearances, so each one lands as "oh no, what does the gauge say?"
- **No padding to hit 15 minutes.** If an episode is 12:38, that's the length.
- **Serious sections:** no music, slow map and counter animation, and nothing played for laughs.
