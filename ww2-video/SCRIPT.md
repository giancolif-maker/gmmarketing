# SCRIPT — World War II, a 3-part series (~15 min each)

**Format:** the Punic War house style. A fast, amused narrator carries the history; characters carry
the jokes in short exchanges (2–8 words a line, punchline last). `[brackets]` are visuals or sound.
**Original jokes only.** These use the joke structures in `punic-war-video/COMEDY_NOTES.md`; nothing is
borrowed from OverSimplified's own WW2 episodes.

**Hard rules for this series**
- **No jokes about victims, ever.** The Holocaust, Nanking, the Blitz, the siege of Leningrad,
  Hiroshima and Nagasaki are told straight: plain sentences, real numbers, no sound gags. The next
  joke only comes after a scene change.
- **Satire aims at the people in charge.** Hitler is petty, vain and furious, never cool. Mussolini is
  vain, Stalin paranoid, and so on. Nobody responsible for atrocities gets a sympathetic punchline.
- **No swastikas or real Nazi insignia.** Germany's flag is a plain black-red bar; armbands are plain.
  This keeps the channel clear of YouTube's violent-extremism filters.

| STALIN | Quiet, suspicious, deadpan. Trusts nobody (except, briefly, Hitler). |
| CHAMBERLAIN | Polite, hopeful, umbrella. |
| CHURCHILL | Growly, stubborn, keeps a tally of "I told you so"s. |

| UNCLE SAM'S SHOP: CLERK | Cheerful American shopkeeper ("We're neutral!"). |
| TOJO / YAMAMOTO | Tojo: gung-ho. Yamamoto: the one worried guy (the Mago role). |
| KLAUS | German quartermaster. Reads the fuel gauge. Always bad news. |
| DE GAULLE | Very tall, very French, very insistent he's in charge. |
| EISENHOWER, MONTGOMERY, PATTON, ZHUKOV, ROMMEL | One-scene officers. |

## Running gags (each one escalates, then pays off)

1. **The "Last One, Promise" form.** Hitler fills in the same form for every land grab ("This is my
   LAST territorial demand"), and Chamberlain stamps it APPEASED. Each one is a template scene with one
   word swapped. Pays off when the form arrives for Poland and the box says "Not the last one."
2. **Mussolini's victory banners.** He pre-orders a VICTORY banner before every campaign; Enzo the
   printer has to fix each one ("VICTORY IN GREECE" → "VICTORY IN GREECE (PENDING)" → "HELP IN GREECE").
   Final banner, Part 3: Enzo is printing something else entirely.
3. **The Churchill Tally.** "Times Churchill said 'I told you so'": a counter in the corner that ticks
   up in Part 1, and finally ticks DOWN once, in Part 3, when he's wrong.
4. **America's shop.** A cheerful store: "WE'RE NEUTRAL! (Payment up front.)" It becomes
   "Borrow it, we'll figure it out" (Lend-Lease), and after Pearl Harbor the sign flips to "OPEN FOR WAR".
5. **Klaus and the fuel gauge.** Germany never has enough fuel. Klaus reads the gauge at every big
   offensive; the needle gets lower each part, and the Battle of the Bulge depends on capturing fuel.
6. **De Gaulle is in charge.** Of France. From London. Nobody asked. He says it every time he appears.
7. **The channel in-joke.** Chamberlain's desk mug: "WORLD'S BEST APPEASER" (cousin of the
   "WORLD'S BEST CONSUL" mug).
8. **Bookend.** The series opens with a corporal angry about a piece of paper (1918) and ends with
   a piece of paper being signed (1945).

# PART 1 — "The Guy With the Moustache" (1918–1941)

## F01 — Cold open: a piece of paper
[Black. A hospital ward, November 1918. A bandaged soldier in a bed. A newspaper slides in: "ARMISTICE".]

    NARRATOR: November, 1918. The First World War is over! Ten million soldiers dead, empires gone, and everybody agrees: never again.
    NARRATOR: This man disagrees. He's a corporal, temporarily blinded by gas, and he's just found out Germany lost.
    HITLER: Lost? We didn't lose. Somebody… made us lose.
    NARRATOR: He was wrong about that. He was going to be wrong about a lot of things. And it was going to cost the world more than seventy million lives.
[TITLE: WORLD WAR II — Part 1: "The Guy With the Moustache"]

## F02 — The bill
[Versailles hall. A giant invoice unrolls down a staircase.]

    NARRATOR: To end the war, the winners wrote the Treaty of Versailles. Germany had to accept the blame, give up land, shrink its army to a hundred thousand men, and pay for everything.
    GERMAN DIPLOMAT: How much is everything?
    FRENCH DIPLOMAT: We'll let you know.
    NARRATOR: It was a hundred and thirty-two billion gold marks. Germany felt humiliated. The French felt it wasn't enough. Everyone felt bad, which is usually how wars start.

## F03 — Money stops working
[A German shopkeeper weighs a loaf of bread against a wheelbarrow full of banknotes.]

    NARRATOR: Then Germany printed money to pay its debts, and money stopped meaning anything. By 1923, a loaf of bread cost two hundred billion marks.
    CUSTOMER: One bread, please.
    SHOPKEEPER: That'll be… hang on, the price just went up.
    CUSTOMER: I've been standing here for one minute!
    SHOPKEEPER: Inflation's very fast today.
    NARRATOR: People used banknotes as wallpaper. It was cheaper than wallpaper.

## F04 — Enter the moustache
[A crowded beer hall. Hitler on a table, shouting. A few people listening, many eating sausages.]

    NARRATOR: That corporal joined a tiny, angry political party, and discovered he had one talent: shouting at rooms full of people until they agreed with him.
    HITLER: And who is to blame for all of this?!
    MAN WITH SAUSAGE: The… sausage price?
    HITLER: NO!
    NARRATOR: He blamed Jews, communists, and the treaty, for everything. In 1923 he tried to seize power by force in a beer hall. It failed, and he went to prison.
    NARRATOR: There he wrote a book full of his hatreds and his plans. Nobody important read it. They really should have.

## F05 — The Depression
[A Wall Street ticker crashes. The line dips off the edge of the screen and keeps going.]

    NARRATOR: In 1929 the American stock market crashed, and took the world's economy with it. In Germany, six million people lost their jobs.
    NARRATOR: Desperate people listen to angry men with simple answers. By 1932, his Nazi Party was the biggest in parliament.
    HITLER: Everything is somebody else's fault, and I'll fix it!
    CROWD: Yay!
    NARRATOR: That's… not a plan. But it got votes.

## F06 — Chancellor
[An elderly President Hindenburg, a group of conservatives in top hats, and Hitler. A "SURE, WE CAN CONTROL HIM" banner.]

    NARRATOR: In January 1933, a group of old conservative politicians had an idea: make Hitler chancellor, and keep him under control.
    CONSERVATIVE: Don't worry. We've boxed him in.
    NARRATOR: Within two months he'd used a fire at the parliament building to suspend civil rights. Within a year and a half, he was dictator.
    CONSERVATIVE: We have… slightly un-boxed him.
    NARRATOR: Opposition parties were banned. Opponents were arrested. And Germany's Jews were stripped of their rights, step by step, by law.

## F07 — The Night of the Long Knives
[A dinner party. Hitler raises a glass. Half the guests' chairs are suddenly empty.]

    NARRATOR: Even inside his own party, Hitler didn't like competition. His street-fighting militia, the brownshirts, had three million men and a leader, Ernst Röhm, who wanted more power.
    RÖHM: The brownshirts should be the new army!
    HITLER: Interesting idea. Let's talk about it at the weekend.
    NARRATOR: On the night of June 30th, 1934, Hitler had Röhm and scores of other rivals killed. Germany's generals were relieved. Germany's army then swore a personal oath of loyalty, not to the country, but to Hitler himself.
    GENERAL: Was that oath… very binding?
    NARRATOR: Extremely.

## F08 — The Olympics
[Berlin, 1936. A huge stadium. Hitler in the stands with a clipboard: "PROOF WE ARE THE BEST".]

    NARRATOR: In 1936, Berlin hosted the Olympic Games, and the Nazis planned to use them to prove their racist theories to the world.
    HITLER: Watch. Our athletes will win everything.
    NARRATOR: An African American sprinter named Jesse Owens won four gold medals.
    HITLER: (crossing things off the clipboard) Everything… except running. And jumping.
    NARRATOR: The famous story that Hitler refused to shake Owens's hand is more complicated than it sounds. Owens later said the snub that hurt was from home: his own president never invited him to the White House.

## F09 — Meanwhile, in Italy
[A balcony in Rome. A man with a huge chin poses. A banner printer, ENZO, at a press below.]

    NARRATOR: Hitler had a role model: Benito Mussolini, who'd been running Italy as a dictator since 1922. Mussolini wanted a new Roman Empire, and he wanted it to look good.
    MUSSOLINI: Enzo! Print me a banner! "VICTORY IN ETHIOPIA!"
    ENZO: We haven't invaded Ethiopia.
    MUSSOLINI: We will. I want the banner ready.
    NARRATOR: Italy invaded Ethiopia in 1935, using poison gas on soldiers and civilians. The League of Nations, which was supposed to stop exactly this, imposed some weak sanctions, and left out oil.
    MUSSOLINI: Hang it up, Enzo!
    ENZO: Hanging it up.

## F10 — Meanwhile, in Japan
[A map of East Asia. Japan's red spreads into Manchuria, then China.]

    NARRATOR: And on the other side of the world, Japan's military had decided Japan needed an empire too. In 1931 it took Manchuria. In 1937 it invaded the rest of China.
    NARRATOR: That December, Japanese troops captured the city of Nanjing and killed tens of thousands, possibly hundreds of thousands, of civilians and prisoners over six weeks. Japan's war in China would go on for eight years.

## F11 — The rehearsal
[Spain, 1936–39. A map split in two. German and Italian planes on one side, Soviet supplies on the other.]

    NARRATOR: In 1936, a civil war broke out in Spain between the elected left-wing government and nationalist generals led by Francisco Franco. Hitler and Mussolini sent Franco planes, tanks and soldiers. Stalin sent the other side weapons.
    MUSSOLINI: Enzo! "VICTORY IN SPAIN!"
    ENZO: It's not your war.
    MUSSOLINI: Put my face on it anyway.
    NARRATOR: For Germany, Spain was a rehearsal. In April 1937, German bombers destroyed the town of Guernica on market day, killing hundreds of civilians. It was a preview of what bombing would do to cities across Europe. Franco won in 1939, and ruled Spain as a dictator for thirty-six years.

## F12 — The "Last One, Promise" form (#1: the Rhineland)
[Chamberlain's desk: umbrella, a mug "WORLD'S BEST APPEASER". A form slides in: "TERRITORIAL DEMAND — Is this your last one? ☑ Yes".]

    NARRATOR: Back in Europe, Hitler started breaking the treaty one piece at a time. In 1936 he marched troops into the Rhineland, which was supposed to stay demilitarized.
    NARRATOR: Britain's policy was called appeasement: give him a little, and he'll calm down.
    HITLER: Last one. Promise.
    CHAMBERLAIN: Splendid! Appeased!
[STAMP: APPEASED]
    CHURCHILL: (from a tiny back-bench seat) This is a terrible idea.
[Corner counter: "CHURCHILL: I TOLD YOU SO — 1"]

## F13 — The form (#2: Austria)
[Same desk, same form, one word changed: "Austria".]

    NARRATOR: In 1938 he annexed Austria, his own birthplace.
    HITLER: Last one. Promise.
    CHAMBERLAIN: Splendid! Appeased!
[STAMP: APPEASED]
    CHURCHILL: Terrible idea.
[Counter: 2]

## F14 — The form (#3: the Sudetenland)
[Same desk. The form now says "Sudetenland (part of Czechoslovakia)". A small Czech official watches from outside a window.]

    NARRATOR: Then he wanted the Sudetenland, a big chunk of Czechoslovakia. So Britain, France and Italy met him in Munich, and agreed to give it to him. Czechoslovakia wasn't invited.
    CZECH OFFICIAL: (knocking on the window) Hello? It's my country?
    HITLER: Last one. Promise. I've said "last one" in writing.
    CHAMBERLAIN: Splendid! Appeased!
[STAMP: APPEASED]
    NARRATOR: Chamberlain flew home waving the paper and said he'd brought "peace for our time." And this is a real thing that happened next: Hitler took the rest of Czechoslovakia six months later.
    CHAMBERLAIN: Hmm. That's… not on the form.
    CHURCHILL: Terrible! Idea!
[Counter: 3]

## F15 — Kristallnacht (straight)
[A city street at night. Broken glass. No music.]

    NARRATOR: In November 1938, in one night of organized violence called Kristallnacht, Nazi mobs burned synagogues, smashed Jewish homes and shops, and killed scores of people. About thirty thousand Jewish men were sent to concentration camps. The world watched, and most countries still refused to take in Jewish refugees.

## F16 — The strangest friendship
[Stalin's office. A framed photo on the desk of Hitler, with a heart drawn on it in pencil… then erased… then redrawn.]

    NARRATOR: Hitler's next target was Poland. But the Soviet Union, run by Joseph Stalin, was right next door. Hitler hated communists. Stalin hated fascists. So, naturally, they signed a deal.
    STALIN: I trust nobody.
    HITLER: Me neither.
    STALIN: Except you.
    HITLER: …Except you.
    NARRATOR: In August 1939, the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact: they promised not to attack each other, and secretly agreed to cut Poland in half.

## F17 — The form (#4: Poland)
[Chamberlain's desk. The form arrives. The box reads: "Is this your last one? ☐ Yes ☒ Not the last one".]

    NARRATOR: The form for Poland arrived.
    CHAMBERLAIN: (reading) "Not the last one."
    HITLER: I'm being honest now. Isn't that nice?
    CHAMBERLAIN: …Not appeased.
[He picks up a different stamp: WAR.]
    NARRATOR: On September 1st, 1939, Germany invaded Poland. Two days later, Britain and France declared war. The Second World War had begun.

## F18 — Poland
[Map: German arrows from the west, Soviet arrows from the east two weeks later. Poland squeezed.]

    NARRATOR: The Polish army fought hard, but Germany attacked with fast tanks, motorized infantry and dive bombers working together. Then, on September 17th, the Soviet Union invaded from the east.
    STALIN: As agreed. Half.
    HITLER: Half.
    NARRATOR: Poland was gone in five weeks, divided between two dictatorships, and its people would suffer under both for the entire war.

## F19 — The Winter War
[Finland in deep snow. White-camouflaged Finnish soldiers on skis. A long Soviet column stuck on a forest road.]

    NARRATOR: Stalin used his half of the deal to grab more land. In November 1939, he invaded Finland, expecting it to take about two weeks.
    STALIN: Two weeks.
    NARRATOR: The Finns had skis, white camouflage, and a lot of experience with being cold. The Soviet Red Army had a problem: Stalin had recently had thousands of its officers arrested or shot, because he trusted nobody.
    SOVIET SOLDIER: Who's in charge?
    OTHER SOVIET SOLDIER: Whoever's left.
    NARRATOR: It took the Soviets three and a half months and well over a hundred thousand dead to force Finland to give up some land. Hitler watched, and drew a conclusion.
    HITLER: Their army is weak.
    NARRATOR: Hold that thought too.

## F20 — The Phoney War
[A French soldier and a British soldier in a trench, playing cards. Nothing happens. A tumbleweed rolls past.]

    NARRATOR: And then… nothing. Britain and France had declared war, and then mostly waited. For seven months. The newspapers called it the Phoney War.
    BRITISH SOLDIER: Got any threes?
    FRENCH SOLDIER: Go fish.
    BRITISH SOLDIER: Is the war on yet?
    FRENCH SOLDIER: We are waiting for the Germans to attack the Maginot Line.
    NARRATOR: The Maginot Line was France's enormous wall of forts along the German border. It was very strong. It was also, crucially, not everywhere.

## F21 — Norway
[A map of Scandinavia. Little German arrows up the Norwegian coast. A British admiral with a teacup.]

    NARRATOR: The Phoney War ended in April 1940, when Germany invaded Denmark, which surrendered in about six hours, and Norway, which fought for two months.
    BRITISH ADMIRAL: We were going to go to Norway!
    GERMAN ADMIRAL: We went to Norway.
    NARRATOR: The British campaign in Norway was a mess. It was so badly handled that Chamberlain lost the confidence of parliament, which, as we'll see, changed everything.

## F22 — Around the wall
[Map: the Maginot Line as a big grey wall. German arrows go around its northern end, through the Ardennes forest.]

    NARRATOR: In May 1940, Germany attacked. Part of its army went into Belgium and the Netherlands, exactly where the Allies expected. And the main punch went through the Ardennes forest, which everyone agreed was too thick for tanks.
    FRENCH GENERAL: Tanks cannot pass through the Ardennes.
    KLAUS: (driving a tank out of the trees) Excuse me, coming through.
    FRENCH GENERAL: …That tank is in the Ardennes.

## F23 — Dunkirk
[A beach. Hundreds of thousands of soldiers. Then a flotilla of small boats: fishing boats, ferries, a yacht.]

    NARRATOR: The German tanks raced to the sea and trapped the British army and many French troops at a town called Dunkirk. So Britain sent everything that floated: destroyers, ferries, fishing boats, pleasure yachts.
    BOAT OWNER: Room for six more! Mind the picnic basket!
    NARRATOR: In nine days, about three hundred and thirty-eight thousand soldiers were rescued. They left almost all their equipment behind. But they were alive, and that mattered enormously.

## F24 — France falls
[Paris. A German flag bar over the Eiffel Tower. Then a tall man in a kepi at a BBC microphone in London.]

    NARRATOR: Six weeks after the attack began, France surrendered. Hitler made the French sign the surrender in the same railway carriage where Germany had signed the 1918 armistice. He'd been waiting twenty-two years to be petty about it.
    HITLER: Same carriage! Same seat! Ha!
    NARRATOR: One French general escaped to London and announced on the radio that France was still fighting.
    DE GAULLE: I am in charge of France.
    BRITISH OFFICIAL: You're in London.
    DE GAULLE: I am in charge of France, from London.

## F25 — Mussolini picks a side
[Mussolini on his balcony. Enzo at the press.]

    NARRATOR: Mussolini watched France collapse and decided it was a great moment to join the winning team. He declared war on France and Britain less than two weeks before France surrendered.
    MUSSOLINI: Enzo! "VICTORY IN FRANCE!"
    ENZO: France already surrendered to the Germans.
    MUSSOLINI: Then print it quickly.

## F26 — Churchill
[10 Downing Street. Chamberlain packs a box. Churchill moves in carrying an enormous cigar.]

    NARRATOR: In Britain, Chamberlain had resigned. The new prime minister was Winston Churchill, the man who'd been saying "terrible idea" for six years.
    CHAMBERLAIN: You were right, Winston.
    CHURCHILL: I know.
[Counter: 4]
    NARRATOR: Churchill offered the British people nothing but "blood, toil, tears and sweat." He also told them they would fight on the beaches, and never surrender. It worked better than it sounds.

## F27 — The Battle of Britain
[Sky over southern England. Spitfires and Hurricanes vs German bombers. A radar tower.]

    NARRATOR: To invade Britain, Hitler needed control of the skies. So in the summer of 1940, the German air force, the Luftwaffe, threw everything at the Royal Air Force.
    NARRATOR: Britain had a secret advantage: radar, which told the RAF where the bombers were coming from before they arrived.
    GERMAN PILOT: How do they always know where we are?
    BRITISH PILOT: Lucky guess!
    NARRATOR: Germany failed to win control of the air, and the invasion was postponed. Forever.

## F28 — The Blitz (straight)
[London at night. Searchlights. Families in an Underground station.]

    NARRATOR: Instead, Germany bombed British cities night after night for eight months. This was the Blitz. More than forty thousand civilians were killed. Families slept in Underground stations. And Britain did not surrender.

## F29 — America's shop
[A cheerful American storefront. Sign: "WE'RE NEUTRAL! (Payment up front.)" FDR behind the counter.]

    NARRATOR: Across the Atlantic, the United States was officially neutral. Most Americans wanted nothing to do with another European war. But President Franklin Roosevelt wanted to help Britain. So America opened a shop.
    FDR: Welcome! We're neutral! Cash only, and you carry it yourself.
    CHURCHILL: We've run out of cash.
    FDR: Hm. How about… you just borrow it?
    CHURCHILL: Borrow tanks? Planes? Food?
    FDR: Borrow, keep, whatever. We'll figure it out.
    NARRATOR: That became Lend-Lease: America would supply the Allies with weapons, food and fuel, and worry about the bill later. Roosevelt called America "the arsenal of democracy."
[The sign updates: "WE'RE NEUTRAL! (Borrowing available.)"]

## F30 — Banner trouble
[Map: Italian arrows into Greece and Egypt, bouncing back. Enzo at the press.]

    NARRATOR: Mussolini, meanwhile, invaded Greece in 1940.
    MUSSOLINI: Enzo! "VICTORY IN GREECE!"
    NARRATOR: The Greeks pushed the Italians back into Albania.
    ENZO: (adding a line) "Pending."
    NARRATOR: Italy also attacked the British in Egypt. The British counterattacked and captured over a hundred thousand Italian soldiers.
    ENZO: (crossing out VICTORY) …I'm going to do this one by hand.
[Banner: "HELP IN GREECE"]
    NARRATOR: Hitler had to send German troops to rescue him, both in the Balkans and in North Africa, under a general named Erwin Rommel. This was going to become a pattern.

## F31 — The strangest flight
[A lone fighter-bomber over Scotland at night. A parachute. A confused farmer with a pitchfork.]

    NARRATOR: And then, in May 1941, one of the strangest moments of the whole war. Rudolf Hess, Hitler's deputy, the number three man in Nazi Germany, stole a plane and flew alone to Scotland.
    NARRATOR: He parachuted into a field, hurt his ankle, and told a Scottish ploughman he had a peace offer for the British government.
    FARMER: Would ye like a cup of tea?
    HESS: I would like to speak to the Duke of Hamilton.
    FARMER: Ye can have the tea first.
    NARRATOR: Nobody had sent him. Hitler announced that Hess had gone mad. Britain locked him up for the rest of the war. And nobody has ever been completely sure what he was thinking.

## F32 — The big mistake
[Hitler at a huge map of the USSR. Klaus beside a fuel gauge reading "½". Stalin, far away, polishing the photo of Hitler.]

    NARRATOR: And now Hitler decided to do the thing he'd always wanted to do. He'd written about it in that prison book: invade the Soviet Union, enslave its people, and take its land for Germany.
    KLAUS: Sir, the Soviet Union is very big. And we have half a tank of fuel.
    HITLER: We'll be done before winter.
    KLAUS: And if we're not?
    HITLER: We'll be done before winter.
    NARRATOR: Stalin received dozens of warnings that the attack was coming, from spies, diplomats and even deserters. He ignored all of them.
    STALIN: Hitler wouldn't. We have a pact. I trust nobody, except him.

## F33 — Barbarossa
[Dawn, June 22nd, 1941. The longest front line ever drawn races across the map. Stalin's photo cracks.]

    NARRATOR: On June 22nd, 1941, nearly four million German and allied soldiers attacked along a front stretching from the Baltic to the Black Sea. It was the largest invasion in human history.
    STALIN: (very quietly) …I trust nobody.
    NARRATOR: And it was only the beginning. Hitler had just started a war on two fronts. Japan was about to start another. And on the other side of the world, a shop that said "neutral" was about to change its sign.
[END CARD: "NEXT TIME — Part 2: Everybody's In Now"]

# PART 2 — "Everybody's In Now" (1941–1943)

## F01 — Cold open: the shop sign
[America's shop. The sign: "WE'RE NEUTRAL! (Borrowing available.)" FDR whistles. A distant rumble.]

    NARRATOR: Previously: Hitler took most of Europe. Britain held on. The Soviet Union got invaded by the one man Stalin trusted. And America ran a shop.
    FDR: Neutral! Still neutral! Everybody okay out there?
    NARRATOR: Not for long.
[TITLE: WORLD WAR II — Part 2: "Everybody's In Now"]

## F02 — Winter is late, then early
[The Eastern Front, autumn 1941. Mud up to the tank treads. Then snow.]

    NARRATOR: At first, Barbarossa went terrifyingly well for Germany. Millions of Soviet soldiers were killed or captured in a few months. By October, the Germans could almost see Moscow.
    NARRATOR: Then the autumn rains turned every road into mud.
    KLAUS: Sir, the tanks are stuck.
    HITLER: Unstick them!
    KLAUS: Sir, the mud has frozen. The tanks are still stuck. But now they're also cold.
    NARRATOR: The German army had no winter uniforms, because it had been planning to be finished before winter. In December 1941, fresh Soviet troops from Siberia counterattacked outside Moscow and pushed the Germans back.

## F03 — The Holocaust (straight)
[Plain, dark background. Simple text: dates and numbers. No characters, no music.]

    NARRATOR: Behind the front lines, Nazi Germany was carrying out the Holocaust.
    NARRATOR: Mobile killing squads murdered Jewish men, women and children in the occupied Soviet Union, more than a million people by the end of 1942. In January 1942, Nazi officials met at Wannsee to coordinate what they called the "Final Solution": the murder of every Jew in Europe.
    NARRATOR: Jews were deported from across the continent to death camps such as Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka and Sobibor. By 1945, about six million Jews had been murdered, along with millions of others: Roma, disabled people, Soviet prisoners of war, Poles and others the Nazis considered inferior.

## F04 — The Atlantic
[A grey ocean. Convoys of cargo ships. U-boats below like sharks. A codebreaking hut in an English country house.]

    NARRATOR: Meanwhile, Britain was an island that needed everything shipped in: food, fuel, weapons. Germany's submarines, the U-boats, hunted the cargo convoys across the Atlantic in packs.
    NARRATOR: Britain's secret weapon was a country house called Bletchley Park, where mathematicians, including Alan Turing, broke the German Enigma code and read many of the U-boats' orders.
    U-BOAT CAPTAIN: How do they keep avoiding us?
    CODEBREAKER: (with a cup of tea) Lucky guess!
    NARRATOR: The Battle of the Atlantic lasted the entire war. More than three thousand Allied ships were sunk, and around three quarters of all U-boat crews never came home.

## F05 — Japan's whiteboard
[A Tokyo war room. A whiteboard. Tojo with a marker. Yamamoto in the back, worried.]

    NARRATOR: Meanwhile, Japan had a problem. It was still fighting in China, and it got most of its oil from America, which had just stopped selling it any, to punish Japan for that war.
    NARRATOR: So Japan's military made a plan.
    TOJO: Step one: take the oil fields of Southeast Asia. Step two: destroy the American fleet so it can't stop us.
    YAMAMOTO: And step three?
    TOJO: (writing) Step three: America… gives up?
    YAMAMOTO: Have you met America?
    NARRATOR: Admiral Yamamoto had studied in America. He warned that Japan could win battles for six months, maybe a year, and after that, he had no confidence at all. He planned the attack anyway.

## F06 — Pearl Harbor
[Sunday morning, Hawaii. Calm harbor. Then planes.]

    NARRATOR: On the morning of December 7th, 1941, Japanese aircraft attacked the US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, without a declaration of war. Eight battleships were damaged or sunk. Two thousand four hundred Americans were killed.
    NARRATOR: But the American aircraft carriers were out at sea that morning. They weren't there. And that would matter a lot.
[America's shop. FDR slowly turns the sign: "OPEN FOR WAR".]
    FDR: A date which will live in infamy.
    NARRATOR: America declared war on Japan. And then Hitler, who did not have to, declared war on America.
    KLAUS: Sir, why did we do that?
    HITLER: Solidarity!
    KLAUS: With who?

## F07 — Everybody's in now
[A world map. Countries light up one by one, like a scoreboard filling.]

    NARRATOR: So now it was truly a world war. On one side, the Axis: Germany, Italy, Japan and their allies. On the other, the Allies: Britain and its empire, the Soviet Union, the United States, China, and many more.
    CHURCHILL: (reading the news) America's in?
    NARRATOR: Churchill later wrote that he went to bed that night and slept "the sleep of the saved and thankful."
    CHURCHILL: Finally. A good night's sleep.

## F08 — The shop becomes a factory
[America's shop sign: "OPEN FOR WAR". The shop grows into a gigantic factory. A worker in a red polka-dot headscarf.]

    NARRATOR: America's real weapon was its factories. Car plants started building tanks and bombers. Millions of women went to work in war industries. By 1944, the US was building a new aircraft every five minutes or so.
    KLAUS: (reading a report) Sir, America built ninety-six thousand planes this year.
    HITLER: Lies!
    KLAUS: It's their own newspaper, sir.
    HITLER: Then it's propaganda!
    KLAUS: It's a photograph of a parking lot full of planes, sir.
    NARRATOR: There was a darker side at home, too. After Pearl Harbor, about a hundred and twenty thousand Japanese Americans, most of them US citizens, were forced from their homes into internment camps, though not one was ever convicted of spying. The US government formally apologized in 1988.

## F09 — Japan's six months
[Map: Japanese red racing across the Pacific: Philippines, Malaya, Singapore, Dutch East Indies, Burma.]

    NARRATOR: For six months, Japan won almost everywhere. Hong Kong. The Philippines. Malaya. And Singapore, Britain's great fortress in Asia, which surrendered with eighty thousand troops, the largest surrender in British history.
    CHURCHILL: Singapore?!
[Counter flickers: "CHURCHILL: I TOLD YOU SO — 4"… "(not this time)"]
    NARRATOR: Exactly as Yamamoto predicted: six months of winning.
    YAMAMOTO: (checking a calendar) …Six months is up.

## F10 — The Doolittle Raid
[An aircraft carrier deck, far too short. A big twin-engine bomber revving at one end.]

    NARRATOR: America needed some good news. So in April 1942, sixteen army bombers, planes far too big for an aircraft carrier, took off from an aircraft carrier, flew to Japan and bombed Tokyo.
    PILOT: How do we land back on the carrier?
    COLONEL DOOLITTLE: We don't.
    PILOT: Ah.
    NARRATOR: The crews flew on to China and bailed out or crash-landed. The raid did little damage, but it shocked Japan's leaders, and pushed them into the big gamble that came next.

## F11 — Midway
[A blue ocean map. Two carrier fleets. A code book.]

    NARRATOR: In June 1942, Japan tried to finish the US Navy at Midway Island. But American codebreakers had cracked enough of Japan's naval code to know an attack was coming, and they needed to confirm where.
    CODEBREAKER: Send a fake message saying Midway is running out of fresh water.
    NARRATOR: Japanese radio repeated the news: target "AF" is short of water. AF was Midway.
    CODEBREAKER: Gotcha.
    NARRATOR: American dive bombers caught the Japanese carriers while their decks were full of planes and fuel. In about five minutes, three carriers were burning. By the end of the battle, Japan had lost four. It never recovered the initiative in the Pacific.

## F12 — Island hopping
[A Pacific map. Arrows hop from island to island, skipping some.]

    NARRATOR: From then on, the Americans fought across the Pacific island by island: Guadalcanal, then the Solomons, the Gilberts and the Marshalls. They skipped the most heavily defended islands and cut them off from supplies. They called it island hopping.
    US OFFICER: Do we attack that island?
    ADMIRAL: No. We wave at it.
    NARRATOR: The fighting on the islands that weren't skipped was some of the most brutal of the war, in jungle heat, against defenders who almost never surrendered.

## F13 — Guadalcanal
[A jungle island. An airfield under construction. Marines in the mud. Ships at night in a narrow channel.]

    NARRATOR: The first big American attack was on Guadalcanal, in the Solomon Islands, where Japan was building an airfield. The Marines took it in August 1942. Then both sides poured in men and ships for six months.
    NARRATOR: Japanese destroyers brought supplies in at night, so fast and so regularly that the Marines nicknamed them the "Tokyo Express".
    MARINE: Is that the Tokyo Express?
    OTHER MARINE: Right on time. Every night.
    NARRATOR: So many ships sank in the channel off the island that sailors named it Iron Bottom Sound. In February 1943, Japan withdrew. It was its first major defeat on land.

## F14 — The Desert Fox
[North Africa. A tan desert. Rommel in goggles. Montgomery in a beret. Klaus with a jerry can.]

    NARRATOR: In North Africa, Rommel, who the newspapers called the Desert Fox, chased the British back and forth across Libya and Egypt. The desert war was mostly about one thing.
    KLAUS: Fuel, sir. We have a quarter of a tank.
    ROMMEL: We'll capture theirs.
    KLAUS: That's not a supply plan, sir. That's a hope.
    NARRATOR: In October 1942, the British under Bernard Montgomery beat Rommel at El Alamein in Egypt. Weeks later, American and British troops landed in Morocco and Algeria. By May 1943, the Axis armies in Africa had surrendered.
    MUSSOLINI: Enzo, "VICTORY IN AFRICA"?
    ENZO: I'm just going to give you a blank one.

## F15 — The awkward handshake
[Casablanca, January 1943. Roosevelt and Churchill in chairs. Two French generals forced to shake hands for photographers.]

    NARRATOR: In January 1943, Roosevelt and Churchill met in Casablanca and announced that the war would only end with the Axis's "unconditional surrender": no deals, no armistices, no repeat of 1918.
    NARRATOR: They also tried to make the two rival leaders of the Free French get along: de Gaulle, and a general named Henri Giraud.
    FDR: Shake hands, gentlemen. For the photographers.
    DE GAULLE: (shaking hands, without looking) I am in charge of France.
    GIRAUD: (shaking hands, without looking) I am in charge of France.
    NARRATOR: The photographers missed it, so they did it again. Within a year, de Gaulle was in charge of the Free French.

## F16 — Leningrad (straight)
[A city in snow. Bread ration cards. A road of trucks across a frozen lake.]

    NARRATOR: In the north of the Soviet Union, German and Finnish forces surrounded the city of Leningrad. The siege lasted almost nine hundred days. Food ran out. Around eight hundred thousand civilians died, most of them from starvation. Supplies came in over a road of ice across a frozen lake. The city did not surrender.

## F17 — Stalingrad: the name
[A map of southern Russia. Klaus points at oil fields in the Caucasus. Hitler points at a city on the Volga labeled "STALINGRAD".]

    NARRATOR: In the summer of 1942, Hitler went south, to capture the Soviet oil fields of the Caucasus.
    KLAUS: Good! The oil! We need the oil.
    HITLER: And also… that city.
    KLAUS: Why that city?
    HITLER: It has his name on it.
    NARRATOR: Stalingrad was an important industrial city on the Volga river. It was also, unfortunately for everyone in it, named after Stalin. Neither dictator was going to let the other one have it.
    STALIN: Not one step back.

## F18 — Stalingrad: the fight
[A ruined city. Soldiers in rubble, fighting room by room. A grain silo. A tractor factory.]

    NARRATOR: The Germans fought their way into the city, street by street, building by building, sometimes floor by floor. Soldiers called it "the war of the rats." Soviet troops held on to a thin strip along the river, with fresh soldiers ferried across at night.
    GERMAN SOLDIER: We took the kitchen!
    SOVIET SOLDIER: We have the living room!
    GERMAN SOLDIER: Who has the stairs?!
    NARRATOR: It went on like that for months.

## F19 — The trap
[A map: two Soviet arrows punch through the weak Romanian flanks north and south of the city and meet behind it.]

    NARRATOR: In November 1942, the Soviets launched Operation Uranus. They didn't attack the Germans in the city. They hit the weaker Romanian armies guarding the sides, broke through, and met in the middle. Three hundred thousand Axis soldiers were trapped inside Stalingrad.
    KLAUS: Sir, we're surrounded.
    HITLER: The air force will supply them!
    KLAUS: The army needs eight hundred tons a day. The air force can deliver about a hundred.
    HITLER: So… nearly enough.
    KLAUS: (staring) One eighth, sir.
    NARRATOR: Hitler forbade the trapped army to break out. In February 1943, what was left of it surrendered. About ninety thousand men went into Soviet captivity. Only around five thousand ever came home. The battle cost the two sides close to two million dead, wounded and captured. It was the turning point of the war in Europe.

## F20 — Dieppe
[A grey beach in France, 1942. Canadian tanks stuck on a slope of round stones.]

    NARRATOR: The Allies had tried a raid on the French coast in August 1942, at the port of Dieppe. Most of the six thousand men were Canadian. Their tanks couldn't climb the pebble beach, and the defenses were far stronger than expected. More than half the force was killed, wounded or captured in under ten hours.
    NARRATOR: It was a disaster. But the lessons, about landing craft, beaches, air cover and never attacking a fortified port head-on, went straight into planning the big invasion.

## F21 — Bombs both ways
[A night sky over Germany. Hundreds of bombers. Then a city on fire.]

    NARRATOR: Britain and America also bombed Germany, the British by night and the Americans by day, aiming at factories, railways and, increasingly, whole cities. In July 1943, a raid on Hamburg created a firestorm that killed around thirty-seven thousand people in a single week. Allied aircrews died in huge numbers too: Bomber Command alone lost more than fifty-five thousand men.

## F22 — Warsaw, 1943 (straight)
[A walled ghetto in a city. Smoke. No music.]

    NARRATOR: In April 1943, when the Nazis came to deport the last Jews of the Warsaw Ghetto to the death camps, a few hundred poorly armed young fighters rose up and resisted for almost a month. The Germans burned the ghetto to the ground. The uprising became a symbol of resistance that has never been forgotten.

## F23 — The Churchill Tally
[Churchill in a bath, working. The tally counter in the corner.]

    NARRATOR: Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin started meeting to plan the rest of the war. Churchill wanted to attack Italy, which he called "the soft underbelly" of Europe.
    CHURCHILL: Italy will be easy.
    STALIN: You've said "I told you so" four times. Will this be five?
    CHURCHILL: Obviously.
[Counter: "4…?"]
    NARRATOR: Hold that thought.

## F24 — Kursk
[A huge tank battle on open steppe. Klaus with a gauge that reads "¼".]

    NARRATOR: In July 1943, Germany made one last big attack in the East, at Kursk, with brand-new tanks. But the Soviets knew it was coming, and had dug minefields and anti-tank defenses dozens of kilometers deep.
    KLAUS: Sir, they have more tanks than us.
    HITLER: Our tanks are better!
    KLAUS: They have a lot more, sir. And they are expecting us. And our tanks keep breaking down.
    NARRATOR: Kursk was one of the largest tank battles in history, and Germany lost. After it, the German army in the East never attacked on a large scale again. From now on, it would only go one way: backwards.

## F25 — Italy wobbles
[Sicily. Allied landing craft. Mussolini on his balcony, then not on his balcony.]

    NARRATOR: That same month, the Allies landed in Sicily. And Italians had had enough. The king and Mussolini's own council voted him out, and he was arrested.
    MUSSOLINI: Enzo! Print "I'm still in charge"!
    ENZO: (packing his things) I've been fired too, sir.
    NARRATOR: Italy surrendered in September 1943. And then Germany immediately invaded Italy, freed Mussolini in a daring glider raid, and set him up as a puppet ruler in the north.
    MUSSOLINI: Enzo?
    ENZO: (sighing, unpacking) …Hanging it up.

## F26 — Tehran
[Tehran, November 1943. The Big Three finally in one room. A dinner table. Stalin's chair slightly bigger.]

    NARRATOR: In November 1943, the "Big Three", Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin, finally met in person, in Tehran. Stalin had one question, and he'd been asking it for two years.
    STALIN: When will you open a second front in France?
    CHURCHILL: Soon.
    STALIN: You said soon last year.
    FDR: This time, a date. May 1944.
    STALIN: (writing it down) I trust nobody. But I am writing this down.
    NARRATOR: The Soviet Union was doing most of the fighting, and most of the dying: in the end, about twenty-seven million Soviet citizens died in the war, more than any other country.

## F27 — Cliffhanger
[A map of the world, late 1943. Axis red shrinking on every side. Hitler's bunker door closing.]

    NARRATOR: By the end of 1943, the war had turned. Japan was losing in the Pacific. Germany was retreating in the East and fighting in Italy. But Hitler would not surrender, and there were still two years, and tens of millions of lives, to go.
    KLAUS: Sir, the fuel gauge.
    HITLER: What does it say?
    KLAUS: It says "no."
    NARRATOR: The Allies had one big thing left to do: open a second front, in France.
[END CARD: "NEXT TIME — Part 3: The Long Way Down"]

# PART 3 — "The Long Way Down" (1943–1945)

## F01 — Cold open: the beach
[A grey sea, before dawn. Thousands of ships on the horizon.]

    NARRATOR: Previously: everyone joined the war, Japan lost at Midway, Germany lost at Stalingrad, and Mussolini lost his banner printer, briefly.
    NARRATOR: Now, the end. It's going to be long, and it's going to be hard.
[TITLE: WORLD WAR II — Part 3: "The Long Way Down"]

## F02 — The soft underbelly
[Italy's mountains. Allied soldiers struggling up rocky slopes in rain. The Churchill tally in the corner.]

    NARRATOR: Remember Churchill's "soft underbelly"? Italy is mostly mountains. Every river and ridge was a German defensive line. The Allies landed in 1943 and fought their way north for the rest of the war, at a horrible cost. Rome didn't fall until June 1944.
    AMERICAN SOLDIER: Soft?
    BRITISH SOLDIER: Underbelly?
    CHURCHILL: …Hm.
[Counter ticks DOWN: "CHURCHILL: I TOLD YOU SO — 3"]
    NARRATOR: The only time the counter went down.

## F03 — Planning D-Day
[A huge map room in England. Eisenhower, Montgomery, a very tall Frenchman.]

    NARRATOR: The main event was the invasion of France. The Allied commander was an American, Dwight Eisenhower. The plan, Operation Overlord, would land five divisions on five beaches in Normandy in one day.
    EISENHOWER: The Germans think we'll land at Calais, where the Channel is narrowest. So we'll land in Normandy.
    DE GAULLE: I am in charge of France.
    EISENHOWER: Great. You can be in charge of it when we get there.
    NARRATOR: To keep the Germans guessing, the Allies invented a whole fake army across from Calais, with inflatable tanks, fake radio traffic, and a real general, George Patton, in charge of it.
    PATTON: I command an army of balloons.
    EISENHOWER: The most important balloons in history.

## F04 — D-Day
[June 6th, 1944. Landing craft. Five beach names appear: Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno, Sword.]

    NARRATOR: On June 6th, 1944, more than a hundred and fifty thousand American, British, Canadian and Free French soldiers landed on the beaches of Normandy, with paratroopers dropped behind the lines in the night.
    NARRATOR: On Omaha Beach, the Americans ran into heavy defenses that hadn't been knocked out, and suffered terrible losses before they fought their way off the sand. By the end of the day, all five beaches had been taken, at a cost of around ten thousand Allied casualties.
    NARRATOR: Hitler had been asleep, and his staff were afraid to wake him.
    KLAUS: Sir? Sir, the invasion.
    HITLER: (from under a blanket) Is it at Calais?
    KLAUS: No.
    HITLER: Then it's a trick. Go away.

## F05 — Rockets
[London, 1944. A small buzzing pilotless plane. Then a sky-high rocket.]

    NARRATOR: That same month, Hitler unleashed his "vengeance weapons" on London: the V-1, a pilotless flying bomb with a buzzing engine, and later the V-2, the world's first long-range ballistic missile, which arrived faster than sound, with no warning at all.
    LONDONER: As long as you can hear it buzzing, you're fine.
    OTHER LONDONER: And when it stops buzzing?
    LONDONER: Then you're not fine.
    NARRATOR: They killed thousands of civilians in Britain and Belgium. Thousands more forced laborers died building them.

## F06 — The bocage, and Paris
[Normandy hedgerows. Then Paris, with crowds in the streets.]

    NARRATOR: Normandy was full of high hedgerows that made every field a fortress, and the fighting there lasted almost two months. Then, in August, the Allies broke out, and the German army in France collapsed.
    NARRATOR: On August 25th, 1944, Paris was liberated.
    DE GAULLE: I am in charge of France.
    PARISIAN: (with flowers) Yes! Yes you are!
    DE GAULLE: (stunned) …Finally.

## F07 — Bagration
[The Eastern Front map. A huge red wave rolls west through Belarus.]

    NARRATOR: Meanwhile, in the East, the Soviets launched Operation Bagration, on the third anniversary of Barbarossa. It destroyed an entire German army group and pushed hundreds of kilometers west in a few weeks. It was a bigger German defeat than Stalingrad.
    KLAUS: Sir, Army Group Center.
    HITLER: What about it?
    KLAUS: It was there. And now it isn't.

## F08 — Warsaw, 1944 (straight)
[Warsaw again. Barricades. Red Army tanks on the far bank of the river, not moving.]

    NARRATOR: In August 1944, with the Red Army approaching, the Polish Home Army rose up against the Germans in Warsaw. The Soviet army stopped on the other side of the river, and Stalin refused even to let Allied planes land to help. The uprising lasted sixty-three days. About two hundred thousand Poles, mostly civilians, were killed, and the Germans destroyed most of what remained of the city.

## F09 — The plot
[A briefcase under a map table. An explosion. Hitler, singed and furious, hair standing up.]

    NARRATOR: By July 1944, some German officers realized Hitler was destroying their country. Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg carried a bomb in a briefcase into a meeting with Hitler, and left.
    NARRATOR: Someone moved the briefcase behind a thick table leg. The bomb went off. Hitler survived, with burns and his trousers in tatters.
    HITLER: I am… fine.
    NARRATOR: The plotters, and thousands of others, were executed. After that, nobody in Germany was going to stop him from inside.

## F10 — A bridge too far
[The Netherlands. A long road north with three bridges. Paratroopers floating down. Montgomery with a pointer.]

    NARRATOR: In September 1944, Montgomery came up with a daring plan to end the war by Christmas. Operation Market Garden: drop paratroopers to capture a chain of bridges across the Netherlands, while tanks drove up a single road to join them, and then on to Germany.
    MONTGOMERY: One road. Three bridges. Sixty-four miles. Splendid.
    AMERICAN GENERAL: And if one bridge goes wrong?
    MONTGOMERY: Then it won't be quite so splendid.
    NARRATOR: The last bridge, at Arnhem, was a bridge too far. British paratroopers held one end of it for days against German tanks, waiting for help that never arrived. The war would not be over by Christmas.

## F11 — The Bulge
[Snowy Ardennes forest, December 1944. Klaus holds an empty jerry can. A fuel depot on a map labeled "AMERICAN".]

    NARRATOR: In December 1944, Hitler gambled everything on one last surprise attack in the West, through the same Ardennes forest as in 1940. The plan was to split the Allies and capture the port of Antwerp.
    KLAUS: Sir, the tanks have enough fuel to get halfway.
    HITLER: Then they'll capture the American fuel on the way!
    KLAUS: This is the North Africa plan again, sir.
    HITLER: And it was a good plan!
    KLAUS: It did not work, sir.
    NARRATOR: The attack punched a "bulge" into the American lines. At the town of Bastogne, surrounded American paratroopers were asked to surrender. Their commander's reply was one word, and it's a real quote: "Nuts!"
    GERMAN OFFICER: "Nuts"? What does "nuts" mean?
    AMERICAN OFFICER: It means "go to hell."
    NARRATOR: The German tanks ran out of fuel. The skies cleared, and Allied planes flew again. By January, the Bulge was gone, and so was Germany's last reserve in the West.

## F12 — The bridge nobody blew up
[A railway bridge over the Rhine at Remagen, March 1945. American soldiers staring at it.]

    NARRATOR: To invade Germany itself, the Allies had to cross the Rhine, and the Germans blew up every bridge as they retreated. Every bridge but one.
    AMERICAN SOLDIER: Sir. There's a bridge.
    OFFICER: There are no bridges.
    AMERICAN SOLDIER: It's right there, sir. It's… a bridge.
    NARRATOR: At Remagen, the German demolition charges failed, and American troops rushed across. Hitler was so furious he had several of his own officers executed. The bridge collapsed ten days later, but by then the Allies were across.

## F13 — The camps (straight)
[Plain dark screen. A gate. Simple text. No characters, no music.]

    NARRATOR: As the Allied armies advanced, they reached the Nazi camps. Soviet soldiers entered Auschwitz on January 27th, 1945. American and British troops reached Buchenwald, Dachau and Bergen-Belsen in April.
    NARRATOR: They found starving survivors, mass graves, and the machinery of murder. Commanders ordered that it be filmed and photographed, so that no one could ever claim it hadn't happened.
    NARRATOR: Six million Jews were murdered in the Holocaust, around two out of every three Jews in Europe. Millions of others were murdered too. Remember them.

## F14 — Yalta
[A palace in Crimea. Churchill, a frail Roosevelt, Stalin, at a table with a map of Europe.]

    NARRATOR: In February 1945, the "Big Three" met at Yalta to decide what happened after the war: Germany would be divided, and Stalin promised free elections in Eastern Europe.
    CHURCHILL: Free elections?
    STALIN: Of course.
    CHURCHILL: Truly free?
    STALIN: (smiling) Everybody will be free to vote for my guy.
    NARRATOR: Roosevelt died two months later. Harry Truman, his vice president, became president, and found out that America was building a secret weapon.
    TRUMAN: Nobody told me about this?
    ADVISOR: It was a secret.
    TRUMAN: I was the vice president!
    ADVISOR: Very secret.

## F15 — Mussolini's last banner
[A lakeside road in northern Italy. A truck. Then a printing press, quiet.]

    NARRATOR: In April 1945, Mussolini tried to flee to Switzerland, disguised in a German army coat. Italian partisans recognized him, and shot him. Days later, Germany's armies in Italy surrendered.
[Enzo, alone at his press, prints a final sheet: "PEACE".]
    ENZO: Finally. A banner that's true.

## F16 — Berlin
[Berlin in ruins. Soviet tanks. A bunker. A red flag over the Reichstag.]

    NARRATOR: The Soviet army reached Berlin in April 1945. Hitler hid in an underground bunker, moving armies around a map that no longer existed.
    KLAUS: Sir, that army is gone. That one too. And that one.
    HITLER: Then who is defending Berlin?
    KLAUS: Old men and children, sir. And me.
    NARRATOR: On April 30th, 1945, with Soviet soldiers a few streets away, Hitler took his own life. The man who blamed everyone else for everything never faced a trial. Germany surrendered unconditionally on May 8th: Victory in Europe Day.

## F17 — The meeting at the river
[The Elbe river, April 1945. American soldiers on one bank, Soviet soldiers on the other, waving.]

    NARRATOR: On April 25th, 1945, American and Soviet soldiers met at the river Elbe, in the middle of Germany. Germany had been cut in two. The soldiers shook hands, swapped souvenirs and drank toasts to peace.
    AMERICAN SOLDIER: Friends?
    SOVIET SOLDIER: Friends!
    NARRATOR: For now.

## F18 — Churchill's surprise
[London, May 1945. Crowds celebrating. Then, two months later, a ballot box. Churchill packing a box. The tally counter.]

    NARRATOR: Victory in Europe Day brought millions of people into the streets. Churchill gave a speech from a balcony to an enormous crowd. And two months later, British voters, ready for change after the war, voted him out of office.
    CHURCHILL: They what?
    ATTLEE: (polite) Hello. I'm the new prime minister.
    CHURCHILL: Nobody told me so.
[Counter: "CHURCHILL: I TOLD YOU SO — 3" … "(nobody told HIM so)"]
    NARRATOR: He'd be back as prime minister in 1951. He was very stubborn.

## F19 — Leyte Gulf
[A huge naval map off the Philippines. MacArthur wading ashore in sunglasses, a photographer walking backwards.]

    NARRATOR: Meanwhile, in the Pacific, the Americans had smashed Japan's carrier pilots in June 1944 at the Philippine Sea, a battle so one-sided American pilots called it the "Great Marianas Turkey Shoot". In October came the battle of Leyte Gulf, the largest naval battle in history. Japan's navy never fought on that scale again.
    NARRATOR: General Douglas MacArthur had been forced out of the Philippines in 1942, and promised, "I shall return." In October 1944, he waded ashore.
    MACARTHUR: I have returned!
    PHOTOGRAPHER: Can you do that again? The light was wrong.
    MACARTHUR: (walking back into the sea) I have returned!
    NARRATOR: That second take never happened. But he really did know how to get a good photo.

## F20 — The war with Japan
[The Pacific map. Iwo Jima, Okinawa. Then B-29 bombers over Japanese cities.]

    NARRATOR: But the war with Japan wasn't over. The battles for Iwo Jima and Okinawa in 1945 cost tens of thousands of American casualties, and many more Japanese soldiers and Okinawan civilians died. American bombers firebombed Japan's cities. A single raid on Tokyo in March killed around a hundred thousand people.
    NARRATOR: Japan's leaders still refused to surrender. American planners expected an invasion of Japan's home islands to be the bloodiest battle of the entire war.

## F21 — Kamikaze
[An American aircraft carrier. A lone plane diving toward it.]

    NARRATOR: As Japan grew desperate, it began sending pilots on suicide missions, deliberately crashing planes full of explosives into American ships. They were called kamikaze. Thousands of young Japanese pilots died this way, and they sank or damaged hundreds of ships. Japan's leaders made it clear they intended to fight to the end.

## F22 — Potsdam
[Potsdam, July 1945. Truman, Attlee, Stalin at a round table. Truman leans over to whisper to Stalin.]

    NARRATOR: In July 1945, the Allied leaders met one last time, at Potsdam outside Berlin, to settle Germany's future and demand Japan's surrender. Truman had just heard that America's secret weapon, the atomic bomb, had been successfully tested.
    TRUMAN: (casually) We have a new weapon. Very powerful.
    STALIN: (casually) Good. Use it well.
    TRUMAN: (to an aide) He didn't even react.
    NARRATOR: He didn't react because he already knew. Soviet spies had been inside the American bomb project for years.
    STALIN: I trust nobody.

## F23 — The bombs (straight)
[A plain sky. Two dates. Two city names. No sound effects.]

    NARRATOR: On August 6th, 1945, the United States dropped an atomic bomb on the city of Hiroshima. Three days later, a second bomb destroyed Nagasaki. Together they killed, by the end of that year, somewhere between a hundred and ten thousand and two hundred and ten thousand people, most of them civilians.
    NARRATOR: On August 8th, the Soviet Union also declared war on Japan, and invaded Manchuria.
    NARRATOR: On August 15th, Emperor Hirohito announced on the radio that Japan would surrender. It was the first time most Japanese people had ever heard his voice.

## F24 — The piece of paper (bookend)
[The deck of the USS Missouri, September 2nd, 1945. A table. A document. Echo of the 1918 hospital scene.]

    NARRATOR: On September 2nd, 1945, on the deck of the battleship USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay, Japan signed the surrender. The Second World War was over.
    NARRATOR: It started with a man who was angry about a piece of paper. It ended with another piece of paper.
    NARRATOR: Somewhere between seventy and eighty-five million people had died, about three percent of everyone alive on Earth. Most of them were civilians.

## F25 — Afterwards
[A new map: Europe split by a line. A big building in New York with flags. A small "UN" sign.]

    NARRATOR: Afterwards, the world tried to make sure it never happened again. Nazi leaders were put on trial at Nuremberg. Fifty countries founded the United Nations. Europe was rebuilt, with American money.
    NARRATOR: And the alliance that won the war fell apart almost immediately.
    STALIN: I trust nobody.
    TRUMAN: Same.
    CHURCHILL: (one last time) Told you so.
[Counter: "CHURCHILL: I TOLD YOU SO — 4"]
    NARRATOR: But that's another war. A cold one.

## F26 — The shop, closing
[America's shop. FDR's photo on the wall. Truman flips the sign to "CLOSED". Then, quietly, to "OPEN (FOR THE COLD WAR)".]

    NARRATOR: The Second World War was the deadliest event in human history. It's worth remembering how it started: not all at once, but one piece of paper, one stamp, one "last one, promise" at a time.
    TRUMAN: (flipping the sign) Closed.
    NARRATOR: For about five minutes.
[END]
