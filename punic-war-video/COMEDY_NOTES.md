# Comedy study — how OverSimplified jokes are built

Source: full caption transcripts of the OverSimplified catalog (studied for technique only).
Rule for our script: borrow *mechanisms*, never lines, names, or specific gags.

## Mechanisms (with how often they show up)

1. **Narrator states fact → characters act it out like idiots.** The scene restates the
   history as a petty, modern argument. The comedy is the gap between epic stakes and
   tiny-minded dialogue. (constant — basically every beat)
2. **The self-aware blunder.** A character *knows* it's a mistake and does it anyway,
   cheerfully. Repeated across episodes as a running bit. (Punic II, multiple times)
3. **Escalating comparison that collapses.** Speaker piles up analogies, runs out, asks a
   subordinate for help, subordinate's answer is dumber. (Punic II)
4. **Mundane modern names for ancient nobodies.** Background soldiers have office-worker
   names; officers get one bizarre fantasy name. Recurring nobody gets a story arc.
5. **Childish register for powerful men.** Consuls/generals whine, tantrum, play
   playground insults, need naps. Power + toddler behavior.
6. **Narrator's vivid "imagine…" paragraph** that escalates in grossness/misery for three
   items, then a deadpan topper. (marsh, Alps)
7. **Anachronistic object/service dropped into the scene** — coffee chains, insurance,
   phone apps, sports — always as a throwaway, never explained.
8. **The literal-minded follow-up question.** A minion keeps asking "and what about X?" to a
   metaphor/speech until the last question is about something embarrassing.
9. **Hard cut to consequence.** Big confident line → immediate cut to the disaster (often
   just a sound effect: screaming, splash) → narrator resumes calmly.
10. **The lie under pressure.** Caught character gives increasingly ridiculous excuses for
    the obvious (bodies under the rug).
11. **Narrator talks to the viewer / insults the viewer** as a bit (sponsor segments, "you
    nimwit").
12. **Tone snap.** Jokes right up to a massacre, then 2–3 sentences played completely
    straight with grim numbers; the next joke lands harder after it.
13. **Callback words.** A single word ("control", "vengeance") becomes the thesis and is
    repeated at the payoff.
14. **Overly emotional/sentimental villain.** The cruel guy is also a soppy dad / cries /
    says "I love you" at the worst moment.
15. **Pet/animal as a character** with its own suffering arc.
16. **Ending on a dramatic tease** introducing the next hero with music sting.

## Pacing
- ~150–170 words/min, narration ≈ 45–55%, dialogue ≈ 45–55%.
- A joke every 10–20 seconds; long straight stretches only around death tolls.
- Dialogue exchanges are FAST: 3–8 lines, each 2–8 words, punchline last.

## Our script vs this (gaps to fix)
- Our narrator does too much; scenes need more back-and-forth dialogue.
- Too few "escalate then topple" beats; too many one-liners that just restate the fact.
- Characters need stronger, dumber wants (Sempronius wants glory, Flaccus wants legions,
  Gisgo wants a nap, Surus wants the beach) so every scene has a petty conflict.
- Avoid overlapping with their specific gags (oiled breakfast jokes, "falling for it",
  farm jokes, "Gisco" name, metaphor gladiator scene, bath excuse).

## More mechanisms (First Punic War I–II, Emu War, Pig War)

17. **Repeated identical exchange as an in-episode callback.** The same 2-line exchange
    (a "fair compromise" offer → instant flat refusal; "Straight line? Straight line.")
    returns 2–3 times; each repeat is funnier because the stakes grew.
18. **"He's the kind of guy who…" triplet.** Character intro by three escalating
    anecdotes, then real historical insult-quotes from contemporaries as the topper.
19. **Two-sided retelling (Rashomon).** Each party narrates the same incident; each
    version is self-servingly absurd; authority figure ends it with a blunt verdict.
20. **Bad-accounting deadpan.** An official reads numbers aloud, a questioner restates
    them one by one ("Meaning…" / "Yep."), the conclusion is obvious failure.
21. **Report read the wrong way round / "Are we the best or the worst?" "Yes."** Confused
    superior can't parse a mixed result.
22. **Mocking the enemy's invention moments before it destroys you.** (dramatic irony)
23. **Laughing at nature → nature answers instantly** (thunder / scream / cut).
24. **Speech that ends terribly** ("Is that the end of that speech?" "Yes." → screaming).
25. **Narrator wistful speculation**: "History doesn't tell us. But I like to think…"
26. **Recurring catchphrase with crowd reaction** (a punishment phrase + cheering kids).
27. **Bathos ending**: the epic crisis was solved by something tiny and practical.
28. **Insult nicknames** a character uses for the other side, repeated as his voice tic.
29. **Kid asks parent about history** framing; parent tells horrors proudly, kid is
    horrified; parent snaps then apologises — repeated beat.

## More mechanisms (Napoleon I–II, Prohibition)

30. **A single running gag across a whole episode that escalates and finally inverts**
    (a character's touchy insistence about one trait; a recurring jab at the viewer that
    ends with a sincere compliment instead).
31. **Format parody**: a historical scene staged as a TV talk show / courtroom / sports
    commentary / infomercial.
32. **"If X, then Y… and if Y, then Z…" chain** spoken at speed until the narrator says
    "I think you get the point."
33. **Boss + long-suffering assistant** double act with a catchphrase dismissal; the boss
    misreads every report to mean what he wants.
34. **Euphemism duel**: villain describes murder with softer and softer words; questioner
    restates bluntly each time.
35. **Cold open sketch** that establishes the absurd normal of the era before the title.
36. **Alliterative / repeated sound-word for an action** used three times in a row.
37. **The surprising real quote** — narrator: "and this is a real quote" — then the
    historical figure's actual words, which are funnier than any invented line.
38. **Self-correcting slip** ("Alcohol is delicious. I mean malicious.").
39. **Ending moral parody**: "So what did we learn?" → three morals, last one nihilistic.

## More mechanisms (Russian Revolution I, Henry VIII)

40. **Bookend frame**: open on the end (deathbed/aftermath) with a question; the very last
    line answers it with the same words.
41. **Ignored-character repeat**: "Who the heck are you?" — "I'm your son." — "I have
    another son?" returns three times across the episode.
42. **Name puns as narrator punctuation** for a character's fate (one per character).
43. **Character tic completed by the other person** — the 4th time, the listener says the
    excuse before the speaker can.
44. **Childhood lesson list reversed later** (the rules he was taught, shouted backwards
    at the moment he turns villain).
45. **Same line, opposite meaning** callback ("You're the king, you can do whatever you
    want" — first as liberation, later as tyranny).
46. **Narrator conspiratorial aside**: "but shh, don't tell him — he's having his moment."
47. **Tour/travel sketch** contrasting countries in one-liners before landing on the
    subject country's misery.
48. **Absurd bureaucratic euphemism for a fatal event** ("a mild case of being blown up").

## More mechanisms (Russian Revolution II, American Civil War I)

49. **Repeated scene template with one word swapped** (a new state/ally walks in, the
    same argument plays word-for-word with new names, ends on the same false promise:
    "this will never come up again." → "A few years later, it came up again.").
50. **Recurring bystander** who gets caught in every battle with a domestic complaint,
    moves away "where the war will never reach him" — and it does.
51. **Bidding escalation**: cautious general keeps raising the number he needs.
52. **Character who desperately wants a cool nickname** and invents his own.
53. **Real anecdote delivered straight** ("what does he drink? … then send him more").
54. **Long list read at speed by an aide** ending with a petty personal disaster
    ("…and your mother-in-law's coming to stay").
55. **"I didn't lose, I merely failed to win."** — euphemistic spin as character tic.
56. **Poison/shot/drowned triple-attempt** escalation with bored assassins.

## More mechanisms (American Civil War II, War of the Bucket)

57. **Pun cascade on a name** (Grant: "I'll grant you that… take you for granted… grant me
    my wish" → "Please stop.") — the victim objects at the end.
58. **The running object** (a bucket) gets absurd devotion in short interstitial sketches
    between the serious history; it pays off as the thing everything is "about".
59. **Myth-busting aside**: "the famous story is actually wrong" — the subversion itself
    is a joke beat, then the real cause is told straight.
60. **Shout-at-God gag**: two sides each claim divine support, ask, and get zapped.
61. **Two identical "You're deposed!" / "No, YOU'RE deposed!"** ping-pong looped 3×.
62. **Tiny-difference tribalism** — a romance destroyed over goblet shapes / how you cut
    fruit; satirises the real feud.
63. **Ending reprises the cold-open line** exactly (bedtime "Say it back!").

## More mechanisms (French Revolution I–II)

64. **Catchphrase verdict spiral** — one official repeats the same sentence ("there's a
    tax for that" / "to the guillotine") to ever-tinier offences, until it lands on a
    child, or on the person who objects, then a sound-effect montage.
65. **Fake vote ratios** — absurdly unfair voting system demonstrated with one dumb motion.
66. **Denial in the face of evidence** — courtier reinterprets each worse report as good
    news, last one physically absurd, then the king flees.
67. **Off-topic sweet aside inside a villain's rant** (talking to a rubber duck) then
    snapping back: "Now where was I? Oh yeah… kill them all."
68. **Character explains own silly nickname origin**, others demand proof, he performs it,
    grudging approval.
69. **Calendar/bureaucracy reform as a domestic argument** (anniversary on the 12th of
    Thermidor).

## More mechanisms (Three Kingdoms, Cold War I)

70. **"See if you can keep up"** — narrator deliberately speed-runs a hopelessly complex
    stretch of history in one breath, then: "All you really need to know is…"
71. **Rigged tally read aloud** ("Communist, Communist, Communist… If that's not free and
    fair, I don't know what is.").
72. **Escalating cover story** for an obviously guilty item ("weather enthusiast" → "very
    naughty weather enthusiast").
73. **Instant replay** of a line in slow motion to expose the blunder hidden in it.
74. **"Let's hope that's the worst of it." → narrator: "It wasn't."** (hard cut).
75. **Accidental early reveal** ("Oh wait, I meant to act surprised. WOW!").
76. **Self-referential callbacks across episodes** — the same throwaway exchange reused
    in a new setting as a channel in-joke. (We can do this *within* our own episode.)
77. **Bloated villain detail as the punchline of his death** ("burned for days. Nice.").

## More mechanisms (Cold War II, American Revolution I)

78a. **Deadpan negotiation that fails in one word** — "Could you please stop?" "No." →
     narrator: "It did not work." Three beats, zero effort; the shortness is the joke.
78b. **Authority ping-pong loop** — "I'm in charge." "No, I'm in charge." repeated until a
     third party walks in and is ignored by both.
78c. **Roll-call with epithets** — a lineup of famous men, each introduced with one silly
     single-trait tag; the last tag is the most mundane.
78d. **Vanity monologue** from the antagonist — he admires himself mid-crisis; aide
     answers every crisis with one bland catchphrase ("that is a pickle").
78e. **Sound-word escalation** — a petty official action (stamping) performed louder and
     more gleefully each time; final beat: someone begs him not to, he does it anyway.
78f. **"I wouldn't…" safety ladder** — narrator lists ever more basic activities you
     shouldn't do near the disaster, ending on breathing.
78g. **Spot-the-X game-show beat** — viewer is asked to find the enemy in a lineup; the
     answer is "all of them" / "none of them".
78h. **Succession blur** — "he got old and died, and was replaced by a guy who got old and
     died, and was replaced by…" said at speed, same drawing reused.
78i. **Betrayal in surfer register** — a historic betrayal answered with "Dude. Not cool."

## More mechanisms (Am. Revolution II, 1066, Football War, Falklands, WW1 I–II, WW2 I–II, Hitler I–II)

79. **Caption heckle** — neutral narration, then an on-screen bracket judges the character in 2–3 words.
80. **Identity as explanation** — "Of course he did. He's [X]." Repeated once as "Again: [X]."
81. **Expectation stated, then done** — "Nobody expected [X]. So he did [X]." The joke is the "so".
82. **Echo with a loophole** — B repeats A's promise word for word plus one clause that voids it.
83. **Ironic "could you imagine if…"** — a character jokingly describes his own real doom.
84. **Invented last item admitted** — absurd final grievance; narrator: "I made that one up."
85. **Fantasy honours cut short** — narrator plans statues and holidays; the news undoes it.
86. **Compressed recap** — a long justification later repeated as three telegram words.
87. **Ping-pong list** — conquered, un-conquered, re-conquered at speed.
88. **Cynical advice column** — "If you're ever the [shady title] of a [failing state]…"
89. **"And repeat."** — show one broken negotiation, then skip the rest with "and repeat".
90. **Idiom with a dark tag** — "…just the toe, for now."
91. **Grammar-maze promise** — stacked negatives that promise betrayal; "Sounds… good."
92. **Descending outcome ladder** — went well / went less well / went really badly.
93. **Coercion as a menu** — "join us or, you know, be invaded"; answers reported as menu picks.
94. **Rebrand ladder with editorial adjectives.**
95. **Sensible-path fakeout** — narrator describes the sane option and its peaceful result… "Except no."
96. **Sarcastic "Great job, [name]."** as a scene button.
97. **Formalized villainy** — rule is "don't be jerks"; villains hold a meeting and vote to be jerks.
98. **Grand monologue, mundane setting** — the reveal that he's at the barber's.
99. **Rhetorical challenge met literally** — "You and what army?" → points → "Fair enough."
100. **Pretend amnesia** — "Who?" "Never heard of him."
101. **Numbered dismissal** — "One: you're insane. Two: you're fired."
102. **Insult boomerang** — insulting someone with your own epithet.
103. **Default response to the wrong stimulus.**
104. **Impatience punished, then reset** to waiting.
105. **Too-precious weapon** — never used; used once, damaged, "so they didn't try that again".
106. **Protest-too-much** — "No problems. None. Especially not [the coming disaster]. Anyway."
107. **"We know." stonewall** — each complaint gets "We know." then a cheerful exit.
108. **Narrator warns the character** — "Hey, [X]? Look out."
109. **Mundane-reason failure** — the secret mission failed because they got bored.

**Rhythm (from these):** a gag every 15–25 s; 2–6 word lines; A/B/A with a 1–2 word button
("Fair enough." "Okay."); the narrator is mostly calm so sudden casual words pop, with big shouts
saved for set pieces; after a punchline he restates the absurd part as a fragment ("In December.").
Atrocities are never a joke setup; jokes resume only after a scene change.
